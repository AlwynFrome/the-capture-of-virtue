# 1.2.2 The Disappeared Victims

One might object at this point that the two cases are fundamentally different in nature and therefore cannot be meaningfully compared: one involves harm inflicted by an external actor, whereas the other involves harm inflicted by an internal political authority. At first glance, such an objection may appear entirely reasonable. Yet upon closer examination, it does not constitute an effective rebuttal of the proposition advanced here. On the contrary, it reinforces the very principle of moral consistency upon which the argument rests. The crucial question is precisely this: what, exactly, could justify placing a criterion above the principle of moral consistency and thereby declaring that these two forms of suffering are morally incomparable merely because their perpetrators occupy different political or social positions? As noted earlier through the analogy of the criminal gang, if one is forced to compare perpetrators who have inflicted different degrees of suffering, the individual responsible for the greater harm would ordinarily be expected to provoke greater moral outrage than the individual responsible for the lesser harm. Yet this is not what we observe in the cases under consideration. The actor responsible for an immense amount of suffering does not merely fail to receive the correspondingly greater degree of condemnation that such a principle would predict. Rather, it is frequently removed from the moral spectrum of comparison altogether: it is not even treated as an agent of harm against the Chinese people in a manner comparable to that in which Imperial Japan is treated. This asymmetry is precisely what the principle of moral consistency is intended to expose. Consider the matter from an ordinary psychological perspective. Would the fact that the person who has subjected you to the most severe abuse subsequently came to live in the same household somehow dissolve your hostility toward that person? If anything, one would expect the opposite. The more severe the threat posed by someone within the environment in which one must live, the stronger the intuitive desire to escape, seek protection, or establish distance from that person should become. The fact that the perpetrator is geographically or socially proximate to the victim does not, in itself, provide a reason for reducing the moral significance of the harm inflicted. Indeed, even if one adopts the conception of government that is deeply familiar within Chinese political culture—namely, that the state should be understood metaphorically as a member of the national family—the argument becomes more difficult to sustain. Within ordinary social and psychological frameworks, violence inflicted upon a child by a member of the child’s own family would not ordinarily be regarded as morally less serious merely because the perpetrator is a relative. If anything, the violation of trust and dependence may render such violence more, rather than less, morally disturbing. External violence can at least be conceptualized as a threat against which members of a community may unite, resist, and seek protection. But when violence is inflicted from within the very structure that is expected to provide protection, the victim faces a fundamentally different predicament: if even the internal source of protection becomes a source of harm, where is the victim supposed to seek refuge?

The same logic becomes apparent when the comparison is extended to the history of Western colonialism. Even if we adopt the lower normative threshold that colonial domination itself constituted a violation of moral principle, however, a further question remains. Is it not nevertheless more consistent with ordinary psychological expectations that an imperial power would primarily direct systematic coercion and violence outward, against foreign populations, while maintaining at least a basic obligation to protect the interests and physical security of its own citizens, than that a political regime would repeatedly launch large-scale campaigns of organized violence against its own population? The distinction matters precisely because the latter involves not merely external aggression but the repeated transformation of the political community itself into an object of coercion. Whatever moral judgment one ultimately assigns to colonialism, a political order that preserves its own population as a protected in-group while exploiting or attacking outsiders conforms, at least in this limited psychological sense, to a more familiar pattern of group behavior than one that repeatedly turns political violence inward upon its own members. The experience of Taiwan provides another instructive comparison. Taiwanese society has not, despite the violence committed by the Kuomintang against the Taiwanese population, abandoned moral comparison concerning political violence. On the contrary, the February 28 Incident and the broader violence of the martial-law period remain subjects of historical remembrance. The persistence of such remembrance demonstrates that recognition of internal political violence is not inherently incompatible with national identity or collective solidarity. The more revealing question, therefore, is what kind of equivalent public remembrance exists in mainland China for those who died during the Great Leap Forward or the Cultural Revolution. Where is the publicly institutionalized day of remembrance for their victims? What corresponding space exists in the national historical consciousness for their suffering? The contrast is striking. The Nanjing Massacre possesses an established commemorative status, while comparable public remembrance of victims of catastrophic political campaigns conducted by the Chinese Communist Party remains profoundly marginalized.

The historical record becomes particularly striking when one considers that the very city that occupies such a central position in the Chinese memory of Japanese wartime atrocities also appears in the documentary record of political violence carried out by the Chinese Communist Party. In a 1951 directive concerning the suppression of counterrevolutionaries, Mao Zedong wrote of Nanjing: “Nanjing is a large city with a population of 500,000 and the former capital of the Kuomintang. The number of counterrevolutionaries who should be executed seems to be more than 2,000. Too few people have been killed in Nanjing; more should be killed in Nanjing!”[^1] The historical significance of this statement lies not merely in the number of people involved, but in the extraordinary juxtaposition it creates. Nanjing is remembered as the site of one of the most notorious episodes of mass violence committed by Imperial Japan against the Chinese population, and the Nanjing Massacre has consequently become one of the central objects of Chinese historical remembrance. Yet the same city also appears in a directive from Mao calling for more people to be killed there in the course of a domestic political campaign. The comparison is therefore not between two abstract and unrelated historical events. It concerns the same city, the same national population, and two different political perpetrators whose violence is nevertheless subjected to radically different processes of moral remembrance. Faced with such a historical record, can one still maintain, without further qualification, that the two cases are simply “different in nature” and therefore morally incomparable? If so, the burden of explanation becomes substantial. What precisely is the morally relevant distinction? Is the suffering of the victims somehow transformed by the nationality of the perpetrator? Does political membership alter the moral status of death, torture, persecution, or mass suffering? Or does the distinction instead arise from a political conception of collective identity in which violence committed by an external enemy is incorporated into national memory as atrocity, while violence committed by the internal political authority is reclassified, justified, forgotten, or excluded from the same moral framework? This, ultimately, is what the principle of moral consistency requires. It does not require that every historical event be identical, nor does it deny that context, intention, institutional structure, or political circumstance may affect moral judgment. It requires something more basic: that the identity of the perpetrator cannot, by itself, determine whether immense human suffering becomes morally salient. If an act produces profound and unjustifiable suffering, moral judgment should respond to that suffering rather than disappear altogether when the identity of the perpetrator changes.

An especially striking formulation of this asymmetry has circulated in Chinese-language discussions outside the mainland internet: if you can kill ten people, you are a murderer; if you can kill a thousand, you become a monstrous criminal; if you can kill ten thousand, you become a conqueror; but if you can kill ten million—or forty million—you become an immortal and “great” leader. The formulation is deliberately hyperbolic, but its underlying irony is difficult to ignore. If the moral status of an act can be transformed so radically merely by increasing the political power and historical status of its perpetrator, then what appears to be a moral hierarchy may in fact be a hierarchy of political legitimacy. The greater the power capable of producing suffering, the more completely moral judgment retreats from the suffering itself. There is, in this inversion, both profound irony and something deeply disturbing: the victim disappears from the moral equation precisely when the perpetrator becomes powerful enough to control the meaning of the violence.

Second, even if we step outside the historical judgments through which Chinese nationalism is ordinarily articulated and instead examine the characteristic features embedded in the reasoning of these nationalists as they respond to contemporary political issues—most notably their internal contradictions and remarkable conformity at the collective level—we can arrive at essentially the same conclusion: the moral foundation they themselves invoke does not, in fact, exist in any coherent form.

Consider, for example, the Taiwan Strait issue, one of the most readily observable contemporary cases. On the one hand, many Chinese people regard Taiwanese people as “compatriots” and “one of us,” and understand so-called “unification” as a powerful realization of this shared identity. On the other hand, they appear largely unaware of—or unwilling to seriously consider—what the war of “unification” they so proudly invoke would actually mean for the people of Taiwan. This is not merely a failure to consider how Taiwanese people themselves would perceive such a war, how they would understand the identity imposed upon them through Chinese nationalist discourse, whether they consent to such a unilateral conception of national identity, or whether they are willing to accept the political order that China seeks to establish across the Taiwan Strait. It also entails an absence of serious consideration of why the cross-Strait configuration has persisted for so long; what specific historical, political, institutional, and social factors have produced such a prolonged and intense confrontation; whether a potential war would be just or legitimate; what consequences it would have for the wider world; which side would ultimately benefit or suffer from it; and how the international community would respond to such a development. Across these questions, there is remarkably little evidence of sustained and substantive reflection.

Even when some individuals manage to overcome the informational barriers imposed by the mainland Chinese internet and gain access to discussions beyond it, they often remain resistant to the knowledge, normative frameworks, and alternative perspectives available from the broader international community. Instead, they continue to reproduce, often with considerable emotional intensity, the same set of values developed within the informationally closed environment of the mainland Chinese internet—values that frequently fail even basic tests of consistency and ordinary logical reasoning—and reproduce them almost unchanged in comment sections concerning these issues. In later chapters, we will examine in greater depth how this mechanism develops and what it ultimately reveals about the nature of such responses—or, more precisely, what might be called “counterattacks.” The crucial point is that contrary or more persuasive arguments can be experienced as intensely personal and tangible forms of hostility directed against the self, even when, from the standpoint of ordinary reasoning, they constitute nothing more than a normal intellectual engagement with a political question. A logical contradiction does not, after all, necessarily constitute a degradation of one’s identity or moral worth.

Returning to the central argument, one can find even more extreme expressions of this phenomenon on both sides of the Chinese internet divide. Statements concerning Taiwan such as “leave the island, but not the people” and curses directed at Japan such as the wish that “Mount Fuji would erupt” are, at their core, manifestations of the same underlying mechanism. Both are presented beneath a moral veneer, yet the relationship between the moral principles ostensibly invoked and the attitudes actually expressed remains impossible for their proponents themselves to clarify. The result is a profound contradiction: they claim to act in the name of peace while remaining largely incapable of recognizing how repeatedly their own attitudes depart from that stated commitment. Even when they somehow overcome the layers of informational and psychological barriers necessary to recognize this contradiction, they rarely display a corresponding willingness to investigate or accept what the evidence might actually reveal. The phenomenon is certainly peculiar. Yet precisely because of this peculiarity, it exposes something fundamental: the supposed moral foundation of contemporary Chinese nationalist sentiment cannot withstand examination at its core.

Another example concerning the Taiwan Strait further illustrates this point. From the standpoint of conventional political psychology, the more strongly a state regards a particular territory as an integral part of itself—as reflected in the official Chinese claim that “Taiwan is an inalienable part of China”—the greater the political responsibility that state and its citizens ought to assume toward the people who actually inhabit that territory. Such responsibility should, above all, entail taking their political preferences seriously. In a functioning society, if it became apparent that its own proposed political authority over, or political objectives toward, a particular territory were characterized by coercion and authoritarianism, while the institutions already established there demonstrated comparatively greater openness, pluralism, and social prosperity, and if the inhabitants of that territory continued to express clear and sustained opposition to the proposed political order, then restraint would seem to be the more coherent response. Such restraint would at least be compatible with the purported concern embedded in the ambiguous but emotionally charged designation of Taiwanese people as “compatriots.”

Yet when the available evidence—whether favorable or unfavorable to one’s prior assumptions—converges upon the same basic empirical conclusion, the response is often not reconsideration but an attack upon democratic institutions themselves and upon the comparatively higher level of social and political order they represent. Academic argument requires neutrality in its formulation, but I must acknowledge that this particular phenomenon evokes in me a profound sense of moral revulsion and intellectual disgust. Consequently, if someone were to reject my argument from the opposite direction, not in order to challenge its reasoning but primarily to rehabilitate or excuse the conduct of these Chinese nationalists, I would not regard such an intervention as an equally valuable form of dissent. Rather, I would regard it as noise that attempts to undermine the normative premise of rational inquiry itself: that arguments should be settled through disputes over facts, evidence, and logic, rather than through struggles for personal vindication, moral superiority, or symbolic victory.

This resembles the way in which dissenters in the Chinese political discourse are frequently dismissed through the abstract and deliberately indeterminate label of “historical nihilism.” It should be noted that this is not a standard academic concept. Rather, it is a political-theoretical term developed and employed by the Chinese Communist Party as a designation for positions that are perceived as disregarding the Party’s purported historical “contributions” while emphasizing the enormous disasters associated with its rule. The relevance of the analogy is not that these two cases are identical, but that both illustrate the same discursive maneuver: when an argument threatens an established moral narrative, the response is to discredit the person or position rather than engage with the factual and logical substance of the argument.

The same conclusion can also be approached indirectly. Rather than examining Chinese attitudes toward different historical and contemporary issues in isolation, we may compare them with the attitudes of other Asian countries and political entities that experienced Japanese militarist aggression. South Korea, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, Vietnam, and others were all affected, in different ways and to different degrees, by Japanese imperial expansion and wartime violence. Yet which of these societies, in the twenty-first century, continues to exhibit behavior comparable to the extreme nationalist acts occasionally witnessed in China, such as destroying Japanese-branded automobiles belonging to fellow Chinese citizens? Is China truly the only society that remembers Japanese wartime conduct, while the rest of the world has simply forgotten it? Or must one instead assume that the crimes committed by Japanese militarism in China were categorically more severe than those committed elsewhere in Asia, or the sovereignty perpetrated against Western nations, and therefore uniquely deserving of an increasingly intense nationalist response decades after the end of the war?

Neither proposition is self-evident. Indeed, the comparison raises a more fundamental problem. If the moral intensity of contemporary Chinese nationalism were genuinely proportional to the historical harm it claims to remember, then its distinctive persistence and escalation would require an explanation that goes beyond the mere existence of historical suffering. The persistence of the sentiment therefore cannot, by itself, demonstrate the existence of the moral foundation that its proponents claim. On the contrary, the contradictions revealed through comparison suggest that this supposed moral foundation has largely dissolved.

At the end of this discussion, however, I would like to raise a more difficult question—one that makes the problem of nationalism considerably more complicated. Everything we have discussed thus far concerning the moral core of nationalism has rested upon a particular premise: that national identity can be distinguished primarily through geographical continuity, rather than being defined explicitly according to the political regime or institutional system governing a given territory. If even this premise must be questioned to some extent, then the contemporary international situation and the nationalist sentiments prevailing within Chinese society become even more difficult to reconcile in their own terms.

Consider the resulting paradox. The Republic of China, whose forces once constituted one of the principal Chinese combatants against Japanese imperialism during the Second Sino-Japanese War, has now become a political entity that Chinese society regards as a target to be eliminated. Meanwhile, the Japanese Empire that figures so prominently in the historical imagination of Chinese nationalism disappeared as a political entity in the middle of the twentieth century. What, then, are contemporary Chinese nationalists actually fighting against? And what, precisely, are they attempting to preserve?

I suspect that they may never be able to provide a coherent answer. More troublingly, the answer itself may be considerably more unsettling than the question.

Why, then, has this entire configuration emerged in the first place? How was it formed? What consequences has it produced? What interests or political functions does it serve? In what direction does it push the world? And what does it reveal about the deepest vulnerabilities, fears, and recurrent psychological mechanisms of human cognition? More importantly, what significance might these questions have for the study and understanding of international affairs and the internal social dynamics of individual societies? Can we identify and extract from this particular case a more general proposition—one capable of illuminating phenomena beyond the Chinese context itself?

It is with these questions in mind that we now turn to the second part of this book.
