When people outside the Chinese-speaking world attempt to understand contemporary Chinese society, they encounter several obstacles that are considerably more substantial than the simple absence of information. The first is an informational problem. Within the English-speaking world, the amount of readily accessible information concerning the internal operation of Chinese society remains comparatively limited, while many direct channels through which such information could previously be obtained have become increasingly restricted as China has moved toward a more closed information environment. The second is methodological. As discussed in the preceding part of this book, relatively simple questions—such as how Chinese young people respond to Japanese animation—can be investigated through conventional interviews or social surveys. Questions of a deeper structural nature, however, are considerably more difficult to approach through such methods. It is one thing to ask individuals what they believe; it is another to determine how a particular value system became distorted, how it is reproduced, and why individuals may remain committed to it even when confronted with evidence that appears to contradict it. Such questions require more than the collection of individual opinions. They require an understanding of the institutional, cultural, and informational environment in which those opinions are formed.

A third difficulty arises from the position of the Chinese people themselves. Living within a society does not necessarily mean possessing an accurate understanding of the society as a whole. This is particularly important in an environment in which the state exercises extensive control over the circulation of information. An individual may possess a highly detailed understanding of his or her immediate surroundings while remaining largely unaware of conditions elsewhere in the country, of information excluded from domestic discourse, or even of the existence of alternative interpretations of events that appear self-evident within the domestic information environment. It would therefore be misleading to assume that simply asking a Chinese citizen what is “really happening” in China will necessarily produce a reliable description of the underlying social reality. This limitation should not, however, be interpreted simply as a personal defect. Individuals cannot reasonably be expected to recognize information that has never entered their field of awareness, nor can they easily evaluate evidence that conflicts with the conceptual framework through which they have learned to interpret the world.

A fourth obstacle is linguistic. Within the broader resources of the international Internet, some of the most detailed discussions of Chinese social problems remain available primarily in Chinese—whether in Simplified Chinese, Traditional Chinese, Mandarin, or Cantonese. For researchers and readers who do not possess these linguistic abilities, a substantial portion of the relevant evidence is effectively inaccessible. Translation can alleviate this problem only partially, because language in this context carries far more than literal semantic meaning. The fifth obstacle is therefore cultural. Even when the linguistic barrier has been overcome, the reader may still encounter a conceptual environment whose implicit assumptions are difficult to recognize from outside. Chinese society possesses a large number of culturally specific references, linguistic conventions, historical associations, forms of humor, and shared assumptions that are often invisible to outsiders precisely because they are so familiar to insiders. Indeed, Chinese people themselves may not always consciously recognize the extent to which these conventions shape their own reasoning, because what is deeply habitual tends to become cognitively invisible.

The difficulty of crossing these cultural and informational boundaries is, in my view, itself significant evidence concerning the depth of the barrier. It is striking that many of the Chinese-language commentators who have sustained serious political or social-analysis channels on the international Internet are individuals with unusually high levels of education, linguistic ability, cultural fluency, and intellectual sophistication. Their very existence illustrates how demanding it can be to translate the internal logic of Chinese society into concepts that remain intelligible to an international audience. I regard myself as occupying a somewhat similar position. Having grown up within Chinese society, I possess access to forms of cultural knowledge, linguistic intuition, personal experience, and observational material that would be difficult for an external observer to acquire. At the same time, my increasing exposure to the international information environment has provided me with a point of comparison that was not available to many of those around me. It is from this position—neither entirely external nor entirely internal—that I attempt to examine the problems discussed in this book.

My purpose in doing so is not merely autobiographical. I want this analysis to be intelligible to readers outside China and, insofar as possible, useful to those attempting to understand the structural difficulties of contemporary Chinese society. At the same time, I hope that Chinese readers, including those who have already emigrated, will take these arguments seriously. Some of the mechanisms discussed in this book do not disappear simply because an individual crosses a national border. Institutional habits, cultural assumptions, and psychological adaptations can persist long after the physical environment that produced them has been left behind. For this reason, I believe that the questions examined here may remain relevant not only to those currently living in China, but also to those attempting to understand the extent to which their own lives—and potentially the lives of their children—continue to be shaped by the social environment from which they originated. Whether the reader ultimately accepts my conclusions is, of course, another matter. I ask only that the evidence and arguments be considered before the conclusions are dismissed.

We will begin with one of the most consequential institutions through which these social mechanisms are reproduced: the Chinese educational system.
