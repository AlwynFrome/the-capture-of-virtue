To illustrate the problem, let us consider a simple hypothetical example.

Suppose you were abducted by a criminal gang. One assailant repeatedly whipped you until your body was covered in wounds. A second assailant not only beat you but also fractured your bones, tore out your fingernails, and subjected you to even more severe torture. You eventually escaped, and every member of the gang was brought to justice. As an ordinary person, how would you compare these two perpetrators?

Most people would not regard such a comparison as especially meaningful. Both individuals are perpetrators, and both inflicted suffering far beyond what any reasonable person should be expected to endure. The moral character of their actions is not fundamentally altered merely because one caused greater harm than the other. Even if one were to reject this intuitive psychological response and instead argue that differences in the degree of harm should influence subsequent moral judgment, the conclusion would still appear relatively straightforward. The perpetrator responsible for the more severe injuries would ordinarily be expected to evoke greater fear, deeper resentment, and more enduring psychological trauma than the one whose violence was comparatively less severe.

If this principle is accepted, then it becomes possible to examine the historical record more systematically. If historical suffering is to serve as the principal basis for determining contemporary hostility, then the relevant question is no longer simply whether a foreign power harmed China, but rather how extensively different powers contributed to that harm. Accordingly, if we begin with 1840—the conventional starting point of what Chinese historiography describes as the “Century of Humiliation”—it becomes possible to compare the various states that engaged in direct military conflict with China and to evaluate, as far as historical evidence permits, the relative magnitude of the damage each inflicted upon Chinese society. Only after such a comparison can one meaningfully address a more fundamental question: if contemporary hostility is proportional to historical suffering, toward which historical actor should Chinese society logically direct its greatest resentment?

I believe this exemplifies one of the fundamental purposes of academic inquiry. When I first conceived this section, my initial intention was straightforward: to identify the states that had engaged in direct military conflicts with China, examine the various forms of destruction each had inflicted upon Chinese society, and compare them in order to determine, on the basis of historical evidence, which state had caused the greatest harm. Yet as I became increasingly immersed in the process of collecting historical materials and confronting the methodological difficulties inherent in such comparisons, I gradually arrived at a different realization. I came to recognize that the notion of the “greatest historical perpetrator” is itself not a factual judgment but a normative one. In other words, the assumption that objective historical evidence alone can identify a single state as the greatest historical aggressor is, fundamentally, a false problem. The following discussion explains why.

When historians compare the destructive consequences of different states’ acts of aggression, they typically consider multiple dimensions, including population loss, economic devastation, cultural destruction, territorial loss, and long-term consequences for national development. Yet this immediately gives rise to a fundamental methodological question: by what standard should the harm inflicted by one state upon another be measured? Even setting aside the fact that historical scholarship continues to debate many of these issues, suppose that we were somehow able to reconstruct every relevant indicator with the highest possible degree of historical accuracy. Even then, one unavoidable question would remain: what, precisely, constitutes “harm” to a nation? More importantly, how should different forms of historical damage be weighted against one another? Without answering these prior normative questions, no empirical comparison alone can establish which state inflicted the “greatest” historical harm. If territorial loss is taken as the principal criterion, then Russia undoubtedly occupies the foremost position. This estimate combines approximately 1.5 million square kilometers of territory (vast regions of Siberia) officially recognized by the People’s Republic of China[^1] as having been directly annexed by the Russian Empire, together with the present territory of Mongolia (approximately 1.564 million square kilometers[^2]), whose independence from China was facilitated through Russian and later Soviet intervention. Since historians commonly estimate the territorial extent of the Qing Empire at its peak to have been approximately 13.1 million square kilometers[^3], the combined territorial loss amounts to roughly:

$$
\frac{1.5 + 1.564}{13.1} \approx 23.4\%
$$

of that total.[^4] If territorial loss alone constitutes the criterion by which historical harm is evaluated, should Russia—and, by historical extension, the Soviet Union—therefore be regarded as China’s greatest historical aggressor? If, instead, the criterion is the establishment of an unequal treaty system together with the duration and scope of colonial domination, then Britain would occupy the foremost position.[^5] Likewise, if the criterion is the large-scale appropriation of cultural artefacts, Britain and France would remain the principal cases.[^6] Only when direct wartime fatalities are adopted as the decisive criterion does Imperial Japan emerge as the primary historical perpetrator. Yet this is precisely the central issue. Should the number of deaths resulting from a single war necessarily constitute the supreme criterion by which the historical harm inflicted upon a nation is evaluated? If one argues that wartime fatalities are inherently more significant than permanent territorial loss, prolonged colonial rule, cultural destruction, or other forms of historical damage, then such a conclusion cannot be derived from empirical evidence alone. Rather, it requires an independent normative justification explaining why one category of historical harm ought to be assigned greater moral significance than another. For this reason, assessments of the historical harm suffered by a nation can never be established solely through the examination of a single historical event or a single empirical indicator. Any such conclusion necessarily presupposes two prior judgments: first, the selection of one particular dimension of historical harm as analytically decisive; and second, the evaluator’s own normative ordering of competing historical values. Without these prior commitments, the claim itself lacks logical foundation. The same principle may be illustrated by a simple analogy. Suppose one wishes to determine who is the “greatest” athlete. Such a judgment is impossible unless the meaning of greatest is first specified. Does it refer to sprinting speed, long-distance endurance, swimming ability, weightlifting strength, or proficiency in skateboarding? Each criterion yields a different ranking because each defines athletic excellence differently. A sprinter may possess the greatest speed—a proposition that is itself an empirical fact—but it does not follow that the sprinter is therefore the greatest athlete in any comprehensive sense. Such a conclusion depends upon the prior normative assumption that sprinting ability should be regarded as the defining criterion of athletic excellence. The same logic applies to historical inquiry. Facts may be measured, but they cannot be ranked until a hierarchy of values has first been established. Historical scholarship possesses no universally accepted standard according to which wartime fatalities are necessarily more significant than territorial loss, cultural destruction, economic devastation, or long-term developmental consequences. Consequently, whenever one claims that a particular state inflicted the “greatest” historical harm upon another, one is already making a normative judgment before one is making a historical one. It was through this process of continual investigation that I myself came to appreciate a more precise and philosophically rigorous way of approaching such questions.

Having established this conclusion, we may now turn to a further question. If many Chinese people assume that there exists an objective standard by which the historical harm inflicted upon a nation can be measured, then they must, implicitly or explicitly, share a common hierarchy of historical values. What, then, constitutes this hierarchy?

The answer is not difficult to discern. As suggested in the preceding discussion, the dominant evaluative framework appears to assign wartime fatalities the highest priority among all possible dimensions of historical harm. Although public discourse in China frequently invokes the brutality and cruelty of war in addition to the number of deaths, such descriptions merely intensify an underlying moral judgment and therefore need not be examined separately at this stage. The essential evaluative principle can be expressed by a remarkably simple formal model. Let H denote the coefficient of Historical Harm, and let D denote the empirical quantity of Deaths. The relationship may then be represented as:

$$H_{JPN} = nD_{JPN}, (n>0)$$

When plotted on a Cartesian coordinate system, this relationship is simply a linear function (Figure 1.2-1).

Within this evaluative framework, the greater the number of people killed by Imperial Japan, the greater the historical harm attributed to Japan. From this premise follows one of the most familiar propositions in contemporary Chinese public discourse: “We hate Japan because Japan killed too many Chinese people.” If this proposition is taken at face value, then anti-Japanese sentiment is presented as a moral response grounded in the perceived injustice of mass killing itself. In other words, the emotion is claimed to arise not merely from national identity or historical memory, but from an ethical condemnation of indiscriminate violence against human life.

Taken in isolation, such an explanation appears internally coherent. Yet genuine moral principles cannot be identified merely by observing how individuals respond within a single context. Rather, they are revealed by the consistency with which those same evaluative standards are applied across different contexts. If an alleged moral principle is abandoned whenever the surrounding circumstances change, then it is difficult to regard that principle as the product of autonomous moral conviction. Instead, it is more plausibly understood as an adaptive response shaped by external circumstances rather than a value freely chosen and consistently upheld. A simple comparison is sufficient to expose this inconsistency (Figure 1.2-2).

Suppose that the very same evaluative framework employed in discussions of Imperial Japan is applied instead to episodes in which the Chinese Communist Party inflicted mass death upon the Chinese population—episodes whose scale or historical duration, according to the argument presented here, equals or exceeds that of the Japanese invasion[^7]. Under these circumstances, the proposed relationship collapses entirely. The difficulty is not merely that:

$$H_{CCP} \neq nD_{CCP}, (n>0)$$

In certain cases, presenting additional historical evidence concerning the immense human suffering caused by the Chinese Communist Party appears not to increase the historical harm attributed to it, but instead to strengthen identification with the Party itself. Paradoxically, as more evidence of historical suffering is introduced, the perceived coefficient of historical harm assigned to the Party may even decline.

This observation gives rise to a deeper philosophical implication. The very justification most frequently invoked to sustain anti-Japanese sentiment—namely, moral outrage at the injustice of mass killing—becomes, upon closer examination, the strongest evidence against the authenticity of that professed moral foundation. For once the same moral principle ceases to operate under comparable conditions, what disappears is not merely the justification itself, but the phenomenon that claimed to be constituted by that justification. This is precisely where the paradox lies. It resembles a ghost suspended over the consciousness of an entire society: overwhelmingly present in appearance, yet when one attempts to grasp its substance, one finds only an empty form devoid of the moral essence it claims to possess.
