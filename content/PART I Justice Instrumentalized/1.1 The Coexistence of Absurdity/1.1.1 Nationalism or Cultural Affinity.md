To begin, let us consider an apparent paradox. Within the Simplified Chinese internet—that is, the virtual sphere inhabited primarily by mainland Chinese users under the regulatory framework of the Chinese Communist Party—a particular discourse concerning Japanese anime has emerged. According to this perspective, the influx of Japanese anime into the Chinese market is not merely a commercial or cultural phenomenon, but rather a strategic instrument encouraged by the Japanese state to mitigate anti-Japanese sentiment among the Chinese population. From this viewpoint, consuming Japanese anime is understood as participating, whether knowingly or unknowingly, in a form of cultural influence, and resistance to Japanese anime therefore becomes a politically and morally justified stance. Conversely, others regard such a position as excessively radical. Given the enormous market share of Japanese anime in China and its long-established role as an integral component of Chinese youth culture, these individuals are more inclined to acknowledge its profound cultural influence while also recognizing its positive contributions. Rather than rejecting Japanese anime outright, they argue that its aesthetic, artistic, and entertainment value should be evaluated independently of historical and political antagonisms. In the earlier stages of these debates, however, the two positions rarely came into direct conflict. Fundamentally, they represented different ways of interpreting the same cultural phenomenon rather than mutually exclusive ideological commitments. More importantly, both perspectives continued to operate within the same overarching framework of a “historical hatred narrative” toward Japan. Because they shared this broader emotional and ideological foundation, there was little practical or symbolic value in confronting one another. After all, no one could compel others either to consume or to reject Japanese anime.

However, as political tensions surrounding the Taiwan Strait intensified in recent years, the uneasy coexistence between the consumption of Japanese anime and the historical memory of anti-Japanese nationalism became increasingly difficult to conceal. An image that circulated widely across the Chinese-language internet outside mainland China vividly encapsulated this paradox: at an anime convention, a young attendee dressed in elaborate cosplay held up a Death Note notebook toward the camera, displaying the handwritten name of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi. The image was striking precisely because it condensed two seemingly incompatible symbolic systems into a single visual performance. On the one hand, the Death Note itself is an iconic artifact of Japanese popular culture; on the other, it was appropriated as a medium through which contemporary nationalist hostility toward a prominent Japanese politician could be publicly enacted. This demonstrates how Japanese popular culture can simultaneously function as an object of cultural consumption and as a vehicle for the expression of anti-Japanese political sentiment. It is this paradox that constitutes the first problem examined in this study: the “Anime Paradox.”

At its core, the Anime Paradox can be understood as an instance of the enduring tension between state ideology and the spontaneous cultural order of civil society. Unlike many other social contexts, however, contemporary Chinese society does not simply privilege one over the other. This is because the state’s ideological apparatus remains deeply intertwined with processes of national identity formation. Consequently, individuals who seek both to maintain a strong sense of national identity and to participate actively in global cultural consumption are compelled to negotiate a compromise that allows these seemingly contradictory orientations to coexist. Such a compromise entails preserving the political imperative of remembering a painful national history while simultaneously embracing Japanese anime as a source of everyday cultural enjoyment. This logic has occasionally been articulated by official actors themselves. As the Communist Youth League of China once remarked on Weibo, a truly responsible young person should both appreciate the richness of Japanese anime and never forget the historical humiliations suffered by China.[^1] Although expressed in a normative register, this statement illustrates how official nationalist discourse and enthusiasm for transnational popular culture are not necessarily perceived as mutually exclusive. Rather, they are incorporated into a shared cognitive framework in which political loyalty and cultural cosmopolitanism are understood as compatible rather than contradictory dispositions. The capacity to reconcile these two orientations contributes to a sense of psychological coherence while simultaneously reinforcing collective solidarity and social resonance. The young cosplayer who wrote Sanae Takaichi’s name in a Death Note notebook exemplifies this broader pattern. His performance illustrates how political expression and participation in Japanese popular culture can be enacted simultaneously without participants necessarily experiencing their coexistence as paradoxical. The paradox, therefore, is most apparent to the observer; for many participants, it has already been resolved through an everyday practice of ideological and cultural accommodation.

At this stage of the discussion, the issue naturally divides into two distinct questions. The first concerns whether the proposition introduced at the beginning of this article is valid. The second concerns the coexistence of two seemingly contradictory attitudes toward Japan within contemporary Chinese society: to what extent is this coexistence rational, what social or political functions does it serve, what consequences has it produced, and whether there exists a more constructive framework through which individuals and societies may understand this complex historical relationship. We begin with the first question: the claim that Japanese society deliberately promotes anime and other forms of popular culture in order to reduce anti-Japanese sentiment within China.

To evaluate this proposition, it is first necessary to examine its logical structure. Implicit in the argument is the assumption that the reduction of anti-Japanese sentiment among Chinese people constitutes the political objective of Japan’s cultural exports, with anime serving merely as an instrument to achieve that objective. If this is indeed the intended claim, then the available evidence does not support it. To date, no official policy documents, government statements, or publicly available records indicate that either the Japanese government or Japanese society has pursued cultural exports with the explicit objective of reducing anti-Japanese sentiment in China.[^2] Even without appealing to empirical evidence, the proposition appears implausible when considered from the perspective of ordinary social behavior. When two parties have experienced a serious conflict, reconciliation is generally achieved through direct or indirect demonstrations of goodwill. One may apologize explicitly, or, even without verbal apology, seek to rebuild trust by consistently demonstrating positive change, acting responsibly, and exhibiting sincere intentions. Such behavior gradually encourages former adversaries to reassess one’s character. By contrast, the conspiracy-like interpretation assumes an entirely different mechanism: rather than improving itself or expressing goodwill, Japan would devote enormous political, financial, and cultural resources to carefully manipulating Chinese public opinion through entertainment media in the hope that Chinese audiences would eventually develop more favorable attitudes toward Japan. Such a scenario requires extraordinary assumptions about both intention and capability while lacking corresponding evidence.

It further raises practical questions regarding cost, feasibility, and strategic priorities. Even if such an undertaking were theoretically possible, it is difficult to explain why a state would allocate immense long-term resources toward influencing a single neighboring country’s public opinion through indirect cultural means rather than strengthening its own society and international reputation more broadly. Nations that successfully cultivate peace, prosperity, and attractive cultural institutions often generate international goodwill without the need for elaborate psychological manipulation.

Consequently, the proposition that Japan intentionally exports anime for the political purpose of reducing anti-Japanese sentiment in China should be rejected for lack of evidence. However, a different question leads to a markedly different conclusion. Regardless of the original intentions behind Japan’s cultural policies, has Japanese popular culture objectively reduced anti-Japanese sentiment among segments of Chinese society? In this regard, the available evidence strongly suggests an affirmative answer. From the perspective of public policy, Japan has systematically developed cultural diplomacy as an element of its foreign policy since the latter half of the twentieth century. The establishment of the Japan Foundation in 1972 marked the creation of Japan’s first dedicated institution for international cultural exchange. During the 2000s, the concept of Cool Japan gradually evolved from an academic discussion into official government policy. Subsequent initiatives—including the establishment of the Japan International MANGA Award in 2007, the appointment of Doraemon as Japan’s first Anime Ambassador in 2008, the integration of popular culture into recommendations issued by the Overseas Exchange Council, the creation of the Creative Industries Promotion Office in 2010, and the establishment of the Cool Japan Fund in 2013—demonstrate a sustained governmental commitment to promoting Japanese culture abroad as a source of soft power. Importantly, however, the official objectives of these policies differ substantially from the conspiracy narrative described above. As Vyas[^3] argues, the primary purpose of Japan's cultural diplomacy is to enhance international understanding of Japan and improve its global image rather than to target anti-Japanese sentiment in any particular country. Likewise, Agyeiwaah[^4] demonstrate that the Cool Japan strategy was conceived principally as a combination of cultural diplomacy and economic development, utilizing anime, fashion, cuisine, and other cultural products to strengthen Japan's international influence.

The more important question, therefore, concerns the actual social consequences of these policies rather than their intentions. On this point, the empirical evidence is considerably stronger. In her study of Chinese and Japanese young adults, Wu[^5] examines whether Japanese popular culture influences Chinese attitudes toward Japan. Her findings reveal that although Chinese participants generally remained well aware of Japan's wartime aggression, exposure to anime, films, music, and other forms of popular culture encouraged many of them to distinguish between Japan's militarist past, the contemporary Japanese government, ordinary Japanese citizens, and Japanese culture itself. Many interviewees expressed a position that may be summarized as follows: they continued to condemn Japanese militarism and wartime atrocities while simultaneously appreciating Japanese popular culture. Wu therefore concludes that Japanese popular culture contributes to the construction of a more positive image of Japan abroad, representing a clear manifestation of soft power. Related findings also appear in research by Zhou[^6] on anti-Japanese sentiment among Chinese university students. Their work suggests that attitudes toward Japan are shaped by multiple interacting factors, including historical education, political discourse, media consumption, and interpersonal contact, rather than by historical memory alone. Exposure to Japanese culture and direct interaction with Japanese people generally moderate hostile attitudes by encouraging more differentiated perceptions of contemporary Japan. Even without reference to academic literature, anyone familiar with contemporary Chinese society can readily observe the remarkable influence Japanese animation has exercised over several generations of Chinese audiences. Its cultural impact is difficult to dispute. The scholarly evidence merely provides systematic confirmation of what has long been observable in everyday social life.

This observation ultimately raises a deeper normative question. Why should preserving hostility toward an entire nation be regarded as an objective in itself? Why should reducing hatred—or even becoming less suspicious—be treated as something requiring suspicion? If another society attempts to communicate friendship or goodwill through cultural exchange, should such gestures necessarily be interpreted as strategic deception? These questions point toward what may be the central issue underlying the entire debate. The most significant obstacle may not be cultural exchange itself, but rather a cognitive framework that assumes every act of goodwill must conceal hostile intentions. Such a framework risks transforming historical memory into perpetual antagonism and making genuine reconciliation increasingly difficult.

More importantly, even if Japan had intentionally sought to reduce anti-Japanese sentiment through cultural exchange or other forms of public diplomacy, the existence of such an intention would not, by itself, constitute a reason for moral condemnation. The intention to reduce hostility between societies is not inherently objectionable; if pursued through peaceful and non-coercive means, it may instead describe an objective that ought to be encouraged. What matters, therefore, is not simply whether Japan may have intended to improve its image or reduce hostility, but whether the resulting effects contribute to greater mutual understanding and peaceful coexistence. The fact that such an initiative may originate from a former adversary does not, by itself, make reconciliation morally suspect. To reject reconciliation merely because it may have been encouraged by the other side would require a further premise: that the value of reconciliation is somehow diminished by the identity of the party that initiates it.

This, in turn, leads us to the second major question of this article: whether there exists a more appropriate and intellectually coherent way to understand anti-Japanese sentiment and, more broadly, the relationship between historical memory, national identity, and international reconciliation.
