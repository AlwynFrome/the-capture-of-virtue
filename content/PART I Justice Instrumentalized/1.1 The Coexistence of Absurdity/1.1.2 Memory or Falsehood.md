Let us begin with a principal proposition: among contemporary mainland Chinese, anti-Japanese sentiment appears to be driven to a considerably greater extent by political mobilization than by spontaneous moral indignation grounded in individual ethical convictions. The following discussion sets out the reasoning underlying this proposition.

To understand this claim, it is useful to begin with a simple observation regarding the dynamics of collective memory. Under ordinary social conditions, historical memory tends to lose emotional intensity as time passes. Immediately following the conclusion of a war, hostility toward a former enemy is expected to be at its strongest. As generations succeed one another, however, the conflict itself recedes into the past, bilateral relations are restored, and antagonistic emotions ordinarily diminish. This process requires no extraordinary political intervention. Rather, it reflects a general tendency in the evolution of human societies. Just as a cup of boiling water gradually cools once removed from its source of heat, collective hostility normally declines as the surrounding social environment changes.

A possible objection is that severe historical violence produces enduring trauma and that such trauma alone is sufficient to explain the persistence of hatred across generations. This argument is persuasive when applied to individual psychology, but it does not readily extend to the operation of collective memory. Individual trauma is retained through personal experience and biological continuity, whereas collective memory must be reconstructed through education, public discourse, and intergenerational communication. No individual is born with direct access to the memories of preceding generations. Rather, historical consciousness is acquired through formal education, family narratives, commemorative practices, and broader cultural transmission. Consequently, the proposition that trauma naturally reproduces itself across generations is difficult to reconcile with the mechanisms through which collective memory is actually maintained. Once the social environment has fundamentally changed, the traumatic experiences of one generation no longer possess an automatic capacity to reproduce themselves in the next. Trauma, in its immediate form, belongs to individuals or to those who directly experienced a particular historical event. If traumatic memory continues to exert significant influence across multiple generations, such continuity necessarily depends upon deliberate processes of social transmission. In other words, the persistence of collective trauma cannot be understood as a purely natural or self-sustaining psychological phenomenon; rather, it is socially constructed and continually reproduced through intentional educational, political, and cultural practices. Recognizing this element of intentionality is essential for understanding the formation of long-term collective emotions.

If collective hostility would ordinarily be expected to diminish as historical circumstances evolve, an important question follows: why has the Chinese case appeared to develop in precisely the opposite direction?

During the era of Reform and Opening Up, Sino-Japanese relations entered a period of remarkable rapprochement. In October 1978, Deng Xiaoping visited Japan following the conclusion of the Treaty of Peace and Friendship between Japan and the People’s Republic of China, marking the beginning of a prolonged period of political reconciliation and economic cooperation. Over the following decades, Japan contributed extensively to China’s modernization through official development assistance, technology transfer, infrastructure projects, industrial cooperation, and investment. At the same time, the Chinese government formally renounced its demand for Japanese war reparations. During this period, overt anti-Japanese sentiment was largely absent from everyday Chinese society. Historical antagonism, while certainly not forgotten, had ceased to function as a dominant source of popular political emotion. This trajectory corresponds closely to what one would normally expect from a society moving toward historical reconciliation. Indeed, even if one hesitates to characterize Chinese society as following the developmental pattern of an “ordinary” society in every respect, it remains difficult to deny that the Reform era temporarily approximated such a trajectory. In this sense, the period provides an observable case in which historical hostility receded rather than intensified.

From the perspective of contemporary China, however, this atmosphere appears almost unimaginable. Within the collective memory that has gradually been reconstructed in recent years, this extended period of Sino-Japanese reconciliation occupies remarkably little public space. For many younger Chinese, it is scarcely remembered as an important chapter in bilateral relations. Moreover, if reconciliation is to be discussed seriously, it is inaccurate to claim that Japan has “never apologized” for its wartime conduct, as is frequently asserted in contemporary Chinese public discourse. As argued earlier, reconciliation does not depend exclusively upon formal expressions of apology. Institutional transformation, peaceful postwar conduct, and sustained diplomatic engagement may themselves constitute important components of the reconciliation process. Nevertheless, even if one adopts the stricter criterion of explicit governmental apologies, the historical record remains inconsistent with the claim that Japan has never apologized.

According to incomplete but widely cited compilations, successive Japanese governments have issued numerous public statements expressing remorse or apology toward countries in East and Southeast Asia since the end of the Second World War. The precise number varies depending on how such statements are defined and counted. For present purposes, rather than attempting to establish a definitive total, it is sufficient to identify a number of prominent statements that were formally issued by Japanese prime ministers or governments and that explicitly addressed Japan’s wartime aggression, colonial rule, or the suffering caused by Japan’s wartime conduct (Table 1.1-1).

These statements do not represent an exhaustive list, nor should they necessarily be treated as identical in scope or character. Some constitute new statements of remorse or apology, while others explicitly reaffirm positions expressed by earlier governments. Nevertheless, taken together, they form a substantial part of the documentary record concerning the Japanese government’s postwar treatment of its wartime conduct.

From the standpoint of the historical record, therefore, the proposition that “Japan has never apologized for its wartime aggression” cannot be sustained as an empirical claim. The documentary record contains multiple formal statements by successive Japanese governments that explicitly acknowledge Japan’s aggression, colonial rule, or wartime responsibility and express remorse or apology in connection with them. Nevertheless, it is important to distinguish between empirical claims and normative expectations. When many Chinese individuals assert that Japan has “never apologized,” they may not necessarily be making a precise historical assertion. Rather, their statement may reflect the perception that Japan has failed to produce a form of political discourse capable of alleviating the resentment that they continue to experience. In other words, what is often presented as a factual proposition may instead express a normative expectation: that the Japanese government ought to provide a form of symbolic recognition sufficient to satisfy their moral and emotional demands.[^6]

One might further argue that, regardless of the number or nature of Japanese apologies, Chinese citizens remain fully entitled to continue harboring resentment toward Japan. This, however, constitutes a different proposition. If a society consciously chooses to preserve hostility toward another nation irrespective of subsequent political developments or acts of reconciliation, then the question shifts from diplomatic history to political psychology. Such a phenomenon would require a distinct analytical framework capable of explaining how collective hostility can be intentionally maintained despite changing historical circumstances. While this is undoubtedly an important subject, it is not the focus of the present chapter.

Returning instead to the historical trajectory, one finds that the turning point occurred not during the Reform era but after the beginning of the Xi Jinping era. Since then, Sino-Japanese relations have not continued along a path of increasing normalization. On the contrary, political tensions between the two countries have gradually intensified. It may be argued that such developments are not unusual. International politics is inherently dynamic, and changing geopolitical circumstances frequently generate new sources of interstate rivalry. From a macro-level perspective, it is plausible that two countries which were once cooperative may later develop significant strategic disagreements.

Yet this macro-level explanation does not adequately capture how anti-Japanese sentiment is experienced by many people in mainland China. Their judgments are rarely formed from the detached perspective of international relations theory. Rather, contemporary political developments are often interpreted through the emotional framework of historical memory. Concepts such as the increasing complexity of international politics, shifting geopolitical structures, or the distinction between historical grievances and present-day strategic competition seldom function as central reference points in everyday public reasoning.

Consequently, among many individuals who express strong anti-Japanese sentiment, relatively few appear to ask whether contemporary East Asian politics differs fundamentally from the circumstances surrounding the Second World War, or whether present-day disputes ought to be evaluated according to different analytical standards. Even when such distinctions are acknowledged—for example, through arguments that advocate “settling old and new accounts together”—historical emotions are rarely suspended in order to examine current political disagreements on their own terms. Instead, contemporary events continue to be interpreted primarily through emotionally charged representations of the past embedded within collective memory.

A simple thought experiment illustrates this point. Suppose all historical knowledge concerning Japan’s wartime aggression were hypothetically removed from contemporary Chinese collective memory—here understood primarily through the history of Japan’s military actions in China, but situated within the broader regional conflict in Asia. To what extent would the current intensity of anti-Japanese sentiment remain? More importantly, how much of the prevailing mode of political reasoning would continue to appear rational once its historical emotional foundation had been removed?

These observations still fail to capture fully what I regard as the distinctive complexity of anti-Japanese sentiment in contemporary China. I therefore conclude this discussion with a single proposition that summarizes my broader argument. I do not doubt that many Chinese are capable of distinguishing historical facts from present-day realities. What I question, however, is whether they are equally capable of separating contemporary political judgment from the historical emotions through which those realities continue to be interpreted.

Even if one were prepared to accept that contemporary Chinese society approaches both history and present-day international politics through a comparatively emotional lens, another question immediately arises. I argue that the issue is not simply the persistence of anti-Japanese sentiment itself, but rather that many Chinese appear to exhibit relatively limited concern for the actual historical conditions under which their compatriots suffered. The following discussion develops this proposition.

At first glance, anti-Japanese sentiment in contemporary China appears both understandable and morally compelling. The underlying intuition is straightforward: those who inflicted profound suffering upon one’s compatriots should not be readily forgiven. Such an attitude may be interpreted as an expression of national solidarity, social responsibility, and respect for historical victims. From this perspective, the continued salience of anti-Japanese sentiment cannot be attributed solely to state propaganda or fabricated historical narratives. Indeed, to state the argument more precisely, many official historical narratives promoted by the Chinese state are not straightforward fabrications in the strict logical sense. Rather, they derive much of their persuasive force from their foundation in genuine historical events. If Chinese society were genuinely indifferent toward the suffering produced by war, political mobilization centered upon historical memory would be unlikely to achieve either broad acceptance or lasting emotional influence. For this reason, explanations that reduce contemporary anti-Japanese sentiment to nothing more than “brainwashing” by the Chinese government appear analytically inadequate. Such characterizations are themselves highly emotional and often obscure rather than clarify the mechanisms through which collective attitudes are formed. As the Chinese legal scholar Luo Xiang has observed in another context, successful falsehoods almost always contain elements of truth. The same logic applies here. Anti-Japanese sentiment in China rests upon historical realities that cannot simply be dismissed. No political narrative can erase the fact that war inflicted immense suffering upon Chinese society, nor can any subsequent reinterpretation eliminate the historical reality that Imperial Japan invaded Chinese territory. Furthermore, as argued earlier in this chapter, the collective trauma that continues to shape contemporary political consciousness is itself the product of sustained social transmission rather than spontaneous historical continuity. Consequently, the emotional foundation of anti-Japanese sentiment possesses an undeniable basis in historical fact. One might even argue that only a society possessing a strong sense of collective responsibility would continue to regard historical suffering as morally significant across generations.[^7] Yet it is precisely at this point that factual analysis gives way to normative judgment. A moral emotion may be understandable without necessarily exhausting the demands of rational inquiry.

Accordingly, we must return to the central proposition introduced above: if contemporary Chinese society is genuinely motivated by concern for the suffering of its historical victims, then to what extent does it actually concern itself with the concrete historical magnitude and distribution of that suffering?
