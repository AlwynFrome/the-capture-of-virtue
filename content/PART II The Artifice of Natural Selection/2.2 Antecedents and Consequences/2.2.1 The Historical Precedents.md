When considering the condition of a contemporary society, one normally examines not only the phenomena occurring within a comparable temporal scale, but also the broader cultural environment and historical context that have shaped the nation or social group in question. In other words, the factors that historically contributed to the formation of a particular people may themselves provide important clues for investigating the deeper causes underlying its manifestations in contemporary society. Yet this approach immediately presents a significant difficulty. History is itself an extraordinarily broad and heterogeneous field of inquiry, while the formation of different states and peoples has involved an immense number of dynamic and interacting factors. The resulting complexity is therefore exceptionally high. An even greater problem concerns how we are to determine whether events that occurred centuries ago have actually exerted an effective influence upon a particular configuration of a contemporary society, and, if so, in what respects and through what mechanisms such influence could have operated. To investigate this problem, we must first establish a necessary premise: there must exist some factors that enable subsequent generations to preserve, in broadly recognizable form, an already existing cultural configuration. Alternatively, the configuration may undergo transformation, reinterpretation, or extension, but there must nevertheless exist identifiable mechanisms through which a particular change can reasonably be attributed to a specific factor or historical event. Without such a mechanism, the mere existence of a similarity across historical periods cannot establish historical influence. This problem is closely related to the distinction between individual memory and collective memory discussed earlier. People have a tendency to project the experiential trajectory of individuals onto objects of inquiry that are not themselves individuals. This tendency becomes particularly problematic when dealing with historical systems. We often assume that because an individual can carry an experience from one moment of life into another, a society or historical system can likewise be treated as though it possesses an equivalent form of continuous memory. Yet the conceptual bridge required to justify such a transfer is rarely examined with sufficient rigor. The finite scope of the analytical framework is consequently overlooked. We must therefore explain why a particular historical problem possesses the evidentiary and analytical legitimacy required for it to be used in explaining the contemporary social object under investigation. Historical resemblance alone is not enough. Nor is chronological succession. What matters is whether there are sufficient grounds to regard a historical pattern as a meaningful precedent for a contemporary configuration. For this reason, I believe that a serious study of contemporary Chinese society can hardly be established while bypassing its history altogether. There are at least two reasons for this.

First, the ruling party of contemporary China, the Chinese Communist Party, has demonstrated a strong propensity to invoke history as a medium of political expression. History is not treated merely as a record of what has already happened; it is repeatedly mobilized as a source of political meaning and national identity. Precisely for this reason, a substantial component of contemporary Chinese nationalist identity derives from a sense of identification with the history of the territory in question. The political meaning of the present is therefore repeatedly constructed through reference to the past. Second, even if we step outside the political narratives produced by the Chinese state and instead regard contemporary China simply as one of numerous political regimes that have existed along the historical trajectory of the Chinese geographical and cultural space, we nevertheless find a substantial relationship between the contemporary political entity and the historical development of this particular geographical and cultural unit. This is not a superficial conclusion. A substantial body of historical scholarship, both within China and internationally, as well as the self-identification of nationalists themselves, provides considerable support for the existence of a long-term relationship between this territory and the political and cultural formations that have successively occupied it.

Of course, as we have already discussed, historical continuity can be produced through the reinterpretation of the past by contemporary political actors. The existence of a powerful historical identity among nationalists therefore does not necessarily indicate that they possess a sufficiently comprehensive understanding of the historical realities upon which that identity is constructed. A sense of continuity may be politically produced rather than simply inherited. Alternatively, historical continuity may emerge objectively through a complex process of historical development, institutional reproduction, cultural transmission, and reinterpretation, in which case no individual member of society needs to possess complete awareness of the mechanisms through which that continuity has been produced. These two possibilities should not be confused. The fact that people believe themselves to possess a continuous historical identity does not prove that such continuity exists in the form they imagine. But neither does the incompleteness of their historical understanding demonstrate that no meaningful continuity exists. We therefore do not need to determine at the outset precisely which factors directly establish the premise of historical continuity. Those factors may instead be progressively identified throughout the course of investigation. The proposition that must first be established is more limited: historical continuity, understood at the level of an observable and historically recognizable relationship, is sufficiently supported to warrant further investigation. That is the premise established above. It is from this point that I approach the question historically.

The reason is that an examination of Chinese history reveals that the cultural valuation of “natural selection” and the aestheticization of “suffering” did not emerge only after contemporary China entered the global capitalist market. They therefore cannot be understood simply as the products of intense modern social competition. There are identifiable elements, whether explicit or implicit, that exist beyond the domain of market competition itself. The same conclusion becomes visible if we consider the proposition, commonly advanced in discussions of modernization, that the development of capitalism will necessarily be accompanied by a gradual process of democratization. Whatever explanatory value this proposition may possess in other contexts, it plainly does not describe the Chinese case adequately. China did not move toward democratization as its capital market continued to develop. Although such a tendency appeared at certain points, it dissipated relatively quickly. Like many other brief manifestations of an outward-looking consciousness emerging from within Chinese history, it failed to overcome the deeply entrenched political system already in place. The implication is not that capitalism has no political significance in China. It is that capitalism alone cannot adequately explain the persistence of the social logic examined in the preceding sections. If the valorization of endurance, suffering, selection, and adaptation existed before modern capitalism, then modern market competition cannot be regarded as their original source. Chinese history contains precedents for this logic that reach back much further.

Ideas associated with a form of “natural selection” can be traced to the pre-Qin period. During the Warring States period, Mencius, one of ancient China’s most prominent thinkers and a major representative of Confucianism, articulated the following proposition: “When Heaven is about to confer a great responsibility upon a person, it first subjects his mind to suffering, his muscles and bones to toil, his body to hunger, and his undertakings to frustration, so as to stir his mind, harden his nature, and increase his capacities.”[^1] This statement simultaneously embodies an appreciation of a socially conceived principle resembling “natural selection” and an aestheticization of suffering. On the surface, the proposition appears capable of describing the experiences of many exceptionally talented individuals. Suffering may test their character, cultivate qualities and distinctive abilities that enable them to survive, and strengthen their resilience. For individuals possessing a high degree of adaptability, painful experiences may not merely fail to destroy them; they may even appropriate those experiences and transform them into stepping stones for further development. There is, however, a profound problem contained within this line of reasoning. It contains a substantial survivorship bias, an excessive optimism concerning individual psychological capacity, and a profound disregard for the lives of the broader population.

When people invoke the proposition that sufficiently capable individuals will not be destroyed by suffering as a justification for requiring others to endure suffering, they do more than invert the relationship between suffering and success. They reason in the opposite direction. They begin to conclude that in order to produce successful individuals, immense suffering must first be imposed upon them. But the causal relationship may in fact be almost the reverse. A genius who emerges from profound suffering does not necessarily succeed because suffering produced his success. He may have succeeded precisely because the immense suffering he encountered failed to extinguish his exceptional capacities. This distinction is fundamental. What truly enables such an individual to succeed is not suffering itself, but the capacity to continue learning from every circumstance—including one’s own mistakes—even while situated in an environment that is profoundly disadvantageous or even extraordinarily dangerous, and thereby to transform experience into new capabilities. Suffering may provide an experience from which an individual learns. It does not follow that suffering itself possesses the power to produce the capacity to learn. The distinction becomes even clearer if we consider a fish. If a fish is placed in a severely polluted river, it may die shortly thereafter. If, however, the fish possesses genetic characteristics that enable it to adapt to such a hostile environment, it may have some probability of surviving. But this is precisely where the fundamental problem lies. One cannot place ten million fish into such a river merely to identify the handful of individuals possessing advantageous genetic characteristics that allow them to survive, while accepting the deaths of the overwhelming majority. The fact that a few fish survive does not retrospectively transform the polluted river into a successful developmental environment. The survivors do not prove that the environment was beneficial. They prove only that some individuals possessed characteristics that allowed them to withstand an environment that destroyed others. The same error occurs when survivorship is mistaken for evidence of successful cultivation. A value system organized around artificial natural selection therefore produces consequences more serious than the immediate suffering it imposes. Its danger lies not merely in the enormous catastrophe that would result if it were generalized. More fundamentally, it legitimizes and normalizes large-scale catastrophe itself. Once survival is interpreted as evidence of worth, the destruction of those who fail to survive can be retrospectively represented as necessary, productive, or even morally meaningful—regardless of the original motivation and regardless of the identity of those responsible for producing the conditions. This is precisely why human society cannot be treated as an unchanging arena governed by the principle of the survival of the fittest.

Human society is instead a domain in which human beings can systematically transform the environments in which they live through communication, conflict, negotiation, institutions, and practical action. Unlike a natural environment, a social environment is not merely something to which human beings must adapt. It is also something they can collectively construct and reconstruct. We must therefore give priority to those options capable of minimizing direct harm. A social order should not require the oppression of the majority in order to produce a small number of exceptionally adaptable and successful individuals. Talent should not have to be “evolved” through a process of natural selection. It should instead be systematically cultivated under conditions of safety and freedom: through the provision of the social and informational foundations conducive to the emergence of talent, together with an intellectual ethos that encourages exploration. Talent is not endurance forced into existence by an unforgiving climate. It is something that can grow naturally and flourish in fertile soil. Even without approaching this question from the perspective of moral ethics, the empirical experience of modern societies has already demonstrated that open institutions are more capable than predatory institutions of cultivating large numbers of independent and capable individuals. Individuals accustomed to surviving in harsh environments do not necessarily wither when the environment becomes warmer; they may instead flourish even more fully. The reverse is equally important. Individuals who might otherwise have developed substantially under favorable conditions may be reduced to obscurity—or may even be denied basic security—because the societies in which they live deliberately construct a field of “natural selection” that refuses to support, cultivate, or even treat them with basic consideration. This does not occur because such individuals lack the potential to become exceptional. It occurs because the social environment and ideological system in which they are situated refuse to sustain that potential, refuse to cultivate it, and in some cases refuse even to recognize its value. The distinction is therefore not between individuals who possess talent and those who do not. It is between environments that allow potential to develop and environments that make survival itself the precondition for demonstrating that potential. Suffering can only ever be suffering. It may become something from which an individual learns. It may reveal resilience that was already present. It may even become, in retrospect, part of an exceptional life story. But it possesses no inherent capacity to transform an ordinary person into an exceptional one. Its immediate function is destruction rather than cultivation.

The idea articulated by Mencius is not the only historical thread through which this logic can be traced in China. During the Song dynasty, for instance, there emerged a cultural ethos encapsulated in the saying, “All other pursuits are inferior; only scholarship is exalted.”[^2] I do not disregard the broader social context of the Song dynasty, including its preference for civil administration over military affairs. Nevertheless, the absolutist logic embodied in the elevation of scholarship above all other pursuits does not constitute an objective assessment of the actual value of different fields of human activity. The problem is not that scholarship possesses little value. Quite the opposite. The problem is that once a particular form of achievement is elevated into an absolute hierarchy of worth, the diversity of human capacities is subordinated to a single socially privileged route. The question therefore shifts from whether scholarship is valuable to what happens when one particular form of value becomes the organizing principle through which people are expected to evaluate themselves and one another.

During the Ming dynasty, the destructive effects of the imperial examination system upon individuals became particularly evident. Candidates were subjected to highly enclosed and harsh examination environments that could directly cause physical harm.[^3] The examination subjects themselves, including the highly formalized baguwen, or “eight-legged essay,” imposed extraordinarily intricate and rigid requirements upon candidates. Such an examination regime necessarily placed substantial constraints upon intellectual development and independent thought. Historical sources further record numerous instances in which Ming literati suffered psychological exhaustion as a consequence of the examination system. The significance of this system lies not merely in the fact that the examinations were difficult. Difficulty alone is not necessarily destructive. Intellectual difficulty can stimulate exploration, deepen understanding, and cultivate ability. The more fundamental question is what kind of difficulty an institution imposes, what kinds of adaptation it rewards, and what conception of human worth it attaches to successful adaptation. When an institution rewards exploration and independent judgment, difficulty can become developmental. When it rewards increasingly rigid conformity to predetermined forms, difficulty instead becomes a mechanism for selecting those most capable of adapting themselves to the institution. This institutional environment also helps explain why the Ming dynasty appears, relative to some preceding dynasties, to have produced fewer forms of exploratory cultural innovation. The flourishing poetry of the Tang dynasty and the highly developed urban culture of the Song dynasty represent forms of cultural production that emerged through relatively expansive processes of exploration and experimentation. By contrast, much of the intellectual innovation of the Ming dynasty concentrated on the synthesis, systematization, and extension of existing bodies of knowledge, as exemplified by works such as Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu) and The Exploitation of the Works of Nature (Tiangong Kaiwu), or on criticism of existing social realities. This latter tendency was accompanied by the flourishing of vernacular fiction, with representative works including Water Margin, Journey to the West, and Romance of the Three Kingdoms. These works should not be reduced to mere products of institutional oppression, nor should the cultural differences between dynasties be explained through a single variable. The point is narrower: the intellectual environment established by an institution can alter not only who succeeds within it, but also what kinds of intellectual activity become comparatively easy, difficult, prestigious, or marginal.

By the Qing dynasty, even much of the prosperity that had survived from the Ming period had largely disappeared. Works such as The Scholars and Dream of the Red Chamber subjected the examination system and the broader system of ritual and social ethics to powerful literary criticism. One episode in The Scholars, for instance, portrays a man who spends virtually his entire life exhausting himself in pursuit of the juren degree, only to descend into ecstatic madness upon learning that he has passed the examination.[^4] There may be an element of literary exaggeration in the episode, but it is nevertheless sufficient to reveal how thoroughly the Qing examination system, together with the feudal social order surrounding it, could erode and distort the very foundations of an individual’s sense of value. During the later Qing period, literary inquisitions became increasingly prominent.[^5] The historical sequence therefore presents us with something more significant than a collection of isolated examples. The examples differ enormously in historical context, institutional form, and political purpose. Yet they repeatedly reveal a similar problem: human capacities are placed within systems that do not merely evaluate what people can do, but establish in advance which forms of ability deserve recognition, which forms of endurance deserve admiration, and which forms of failure can be treated as acceptable collateral consequences of selection.

At this point, one might raise an important objection: perhaps ancient societies simply lacked sufficient productive capacity to establish open political systems or to provide their populations with adequate protection of their rights and dignity. In ordinary societies, such an argument may indeed possess some validity. Economic development does affect the extent to which individual rights can be institutionally protected, and ancient societies faced material constraints that modern societies do not. But when we turn our attention to the present, the problem becomes considerably more difficult to explain. Contemporary China, after integrating into the global capitalist market, possesses productive capacities far greater than those available at any previous point in its history. If contemporary Chinese educational institutions and value systems are compared respectively with the historical cases described above, however, their underlying logic—and, in many respects, even their outward manifestations—can appear strikingly similar. This creates an important explanatory problem. The increase in productive capacity has not produced a corresponding transformation in the educational values of Chinese society. If the harshness of the earlier systems could be explained primarily by material scarcity, then the substantial expansion of contemporary productive capacity should, at least in principle, weaken the necessity of reproducing those patterns. Yet this has not occurred to the extent that such a material explanation would lead us to expect. We must consequently seek explanations that extend beyond the limitations imposed by economic development alone. Why is it that an educational or administrative logic that existed more than two thousand years ago—one that at times sought to subject the human body and will to destructive pressures—could traverse such an enormous historical timescale and continue to exist with remarkable vitality within a Chinese society of the twenty-first century? This question should not be answered by simply declaring that “Chinese culture has remained unchanged.” Such a claim would be too crude to survive serious historical scrutiny. Nor should we assume that an ancient idea must have remained continuously intact in order to influence the present. Historical systems do not survive merely by remaining unchanged. They survive, when they survive at all, through transformation, reinterpretation, institutional reconstruction, selective preservation, and adaptation to new conditions. The relevant question is therefore not whether the contemporary Chinese educational system is literally the same as the institutions of ancient China. It plainly is not. The question is whether some of the underlying valuations that made those institutions intelligible—particularly the elevation of endurance, disciplined adaptation, suffering, hierarchy, and competitive selection—have repeatedly found institutional expression and therefore remained historically available for reconstruction under new conditions. That is a substantially more limited claim than uninterrupted cultural continuity, but it is also a more defensible one.

There are, of course, many additional conditions and contextual variables that should be incorporated into a fuller examination of the historical materials presented above. The cases discussed here should therefore not be regarded as sufficient, in isolation, to establish a complete causal account. A historical precedent does not become a direct cause merely because a similar pattern appears again centuries later. Nor does recurrence by itself tell us whether the relevant mechanism was cultural transmission, institutional inheritance, independent reconstruction, political reinterpretation, or some combination of these processes. Nevertheless, taken together, the historical materials yield one conclusion that is difficult to dismiss. The practice of constructing an artificial field of “natural selection” has appeared repeatedly on Chinese soil. Across different historical periods, suffering, endurance, hierarchy, disciplined adaptation, and selection have at various times been assigned positive cultural or institutional meaning. These manifestations were not identical, and they should not be collapsed into a single uninterrupted tradition. But their recurrence establishes that the contemporary valorization of competitive endurance cannot be treated as an entirely novel response to modern capitalism. The historical record therefore does not prove that contemporary China is simply the continuation of ancient China. It establishes something more important for the argument of this book: the contemporary system has historical precedents. And once such precedents are established, history can no longer be treated as merely a background narrative external to the contemporary problem. It becomes part of the explanatory field within which that problem must be understood. The question is consequently no longer whether history “caused” contemporary China in some singular or linear sense. The more meaningful question is how historical patterns become available to later institutions: how they are remembered, forgotten, reinterpreted, institutionalized, discarded, and reconstructed; how an old valuation can acquire a new function; and how practices developed under radically different material conditions can become intelligible again within a modern society. The contemporary educational system may shape the roads through which different people move, but those roads did not appear from nowhere. They have histories. And if some of the same principles of selection, endurance, hierarchy, and suffering have repeatedly been embedded within the institutions through which generations of people were educated and evaluated, then the contemporary landscape cannot be understood solely by examining the immediate pressures of the present. The historical precedents do not by themselves complete the explanation. But they establish why the explanation must continue to look backward.
