While writing this section, I repeatedly found myself confronted with a fundamental question: how, precisely, should I describe the problems of China’s existing educational system? The conventional approach would be to describe its observable reality directly—for example, by presenting the concrete experiences of an individual student or examining the specific rules and institutional arrangements of a particular school as representative cases. Such an approach can certainly demonstrate the problems by presenting them directly. Yet for the purposes of the present book, I do not consider it the most appropriate method. First, it would require a large body of firsthand sources, together with systematic analysis and comparison. It would amount to an attempt to reconstruct, from the ground up, a comprehensive portrait of the actual Chinese educational system. For the purposes of this book, however, such an undertaking would be unnecessarily cumbersome, and it is not the type of research methodology I wish to pursue. More importantly, I believe there is a more effective way to depict the characteristics and underlying structure of Chinese education: indirect description. Rather than attempting to catalogue every institutional feature, I will examine several geographically and socially distinct manifestations of the system and ask whether they reveal a sufficiently consistent underlying logic. If they do, then the problem is no longer simply the existence of particular dysfunctional schools. It becomes a question concerning the structure within which those schools operate.

To begin with, we must establish a fundamental feature of Chinese education: despite substantial differences in geography, wealth, institutional resources, and local educational culture, schools throughout China operate within a broadly unified state-led framework. A high-school entrance examination conducted in Beijing and a university entrance examination conducted in Guangdong obviously differ in their immediate institutional form, just as a public school in a small county may differ enormously in resources and atmosphere from a private international school in Shanghai. Yet these differences do not necessarily imply fundamentally different educational logics. The degree to which particular pressures are expressed may vary; the mechanisms through which they are implemented may vary; and the resources available to individual students may vary dramatically. Nevertheless, the institutions remain embedded within a common political and administrative environment. This makes it possible, with appropriate caution, to treat individual schools and educational experiences as windows into a broader institutional structure rather than as isolated anomalies. The proposition I am interested in establishing is therefore not that every Chinese school is identical, but that the most consequential features of the educational system are sufficiently widespread that they cannot plausibly be reduced to the peculiarities of one city, one province, or one institution.

This conclusion is not derived from a single source. Rather, it emerges from the convergence of a large body of material produced both within and outside mainland China. What these sources repeatedly criticize is not merely the educational policy of Beijing, Wuhan, or Shandong in isolation, but recurring characteristics of Chinese education across geographically and socially distinct environments. For readers who wish to investigate this proposition independently, I would recommend two particularly revealing forms of evidence. The first is the Chinese domestic Internet itself, particularly Bilibili[^1]. A reader can access the platform and search for the four characters “中式教育,” meaning the “Chinese educational model.” Bilibili operates within the mainland Chinese information environment and is designed primarily for a Chinese-speaking domestic audience; in this respect, it can reasonably be regarded as one of the closest Chinese equivalents to YouTube. For readers who do not understand Simplified Chinese, this immediately creates a substantial barrier. Yet for those who can cross it, an important phenomenon becomes visible: Chinese users themselves engage in extensive and often intense criticism of their own educational system. This is consistent with a phenomenon discussed in the preceding part of this book in relation to the Anime Paradox. The Chinese state does not necessarily eliminate every form of criticism concerning every social problem. It may instead tolerate, selectively permit, or simply fail to suppress forms of negative expression that accurately reflect identifiable social problems. The existence of such criticism on a domestic platform is therefore significant precisely because it does not originate exclusively from external observers attempting to impose an interpretation upon Chinese society.

Yet even overcoming the linguistic barrier does not necessarily mean overcoming the cultural one. The Simplified Chinese Internet contains an enormous repertoire of implicit references that can be difficult for outsiders to decode: homophonic wordplay, contemporary memes, visually similar forms of Simplified Chinese characters, recurring linguistic conventions, shared historical references, and collectively reproduced interpretations of particular international events. These forms of communication frequently depend upon knowledge that is not explicitly stated because the intended audience is already presumed to possess it. Translation can therefore convey the literal meaning of a sentence while leaving its social meaning almost entirely intact and inaccessible. Although platforms such as Bilibili have introduced translation functionality, translation alone is scarcely sufficient to bridge such a vast and rapidly evolving cultural system. Indeed, even many Chinese users themselves express a degree of exhaustion at the speed with which new memes are generated, transformed, and recursively reproduced. This phenomenon is not unique to China. Digital platforms throughout the world are subject to similar pressures: algorithmic recommendation systems reward novelty, emotional salience, rapid circulation, and highly consumable forms of content. Yet the phenomenon appears particularly pronounced within the Simplified Chinese Internet, where online participation has become deeply intertwined with forms of collective identity and social belonging.

The importance of this phenomenon should not be underestimated. Having spent much of my life immersed in the Simplified Chinese Internet while becoming increasingly familiar with the international Internet in recent years, I have come to regard the former not merely as an information source but as a social environment in its own right. Features such as danmu—the real-time comments that appear directly over video content—and highly active comment sections transform the act of consuming media into a form of collective participation. The individual is rarely positioned as a completely isolated viewer; instead, the experience of watching a video is accompanied by continuous awareness of how other members of the audience are reacting to it. This creates a powerful sense of simultaneous participation and belonging. I would therefore caution international readers against interpreting Chinese Internet culture simply through the content of individual videos. The social mechanisms surrounding that content are equally important. The distinction becomes even clearer when one considers the temporal structure of online information. On the Simplified Chinese Internet, it is comparatively uncommon for a platform to recommend a video published five or six years earlier, let alone more than a decade ago, as though its continued relevance were entirely unremarkable. On YouTube, by contrast, such recommendations are comparatively ordinary. This difference is not merely a question of whether Chinese-produced content possesses long-term value. It reveals something about how users are conditioned to understand the relationship between informational novelty, expected relevance, cultural meaning, and quality. More broadly, it points toward the degree of temporal compression and social restlessness embedded within the contemporary Chinese information environment.

The second source of evidence is a specific analysis produced by an overseas Chinese-language political commentator. In the video, the creator describes what happened after publishing an open call for submissions concerning problems within Chinese education. He reportedly received several hundred submissions from parents, students, teachers, and people who had already graduated from the education system. As the creator himself observed, “several of them were more than a thousand characters long,” while the submissions came from Jiangsu, Henan, Chongqing, Hebei, Fujian, Guangdong, and other parts of China, yet were all “largely the same.”[^2] The significance of this material does not lie in any individual testimony. Individual testimonies are inevitably limited by memory, selection effects, and personal circumstances. Rather, its value lies in the striking consistency of experiences emerging from geographically distant regions and from different positions within the educational system. Parents, students, teachers, and former students occupy different institutional positions and possess different incentives to describe their experiences in particular ways. When their accounts nevertheless converge upon similar descriptions of educational pressure and institutional practice, the resulting pattern becomes considerably more difficult to dismiss as the product of one particular school or locality.

Taken together, these two channels of evidence support the proposition established above: the problems of China’s educational system are not merely local anomalies. They exhibit a considerable degree of generality, consistency, public recognizability, and severity. More importantly, they demonstrate why it is unnecessary for this book to reproduce the entire institutional landscape of Chinese education through an exhaustive catalogue of statistics, regulations, and individual cases. If similar patterns repeatedly emerge from geographically and socially disparate sources, then the more consequential question is no longer simply what does the Chinese educational system look like? The more important question is what kind of person does such a system gradually produce? Once this proposition has been established, we can move beyond the attempt to describe the system entirely from the outside. Instead, we can examine its underlying logic through a case that I know particularly well: my own experience within it.

My own experience provides an unusual vantage point from which to examine this question. Based on my recollection of the social environment in which I was raised, together with the information I have subsequently been able to collect, compare, and integrate, I have reached a tentative conclusion concerning my family’s socioeconomic position within Chinese society: we belonged, by any reasonable standard, to a relatively affluent segment of the population. I do not make this claim merely as a retrospective characterization of my childhood. Several concrete features of my upbringing provide some indication of the material circumstances in which I was raised. From childhood onward, I attended private international schools. During my primary-school years, the annual tuition fees, as I remember them, were approximately RMB 60,000 to RMB 80,000, equivalent to roughly USD 9,000 to USD 12,000 at the 2018 exchange rate. Such expenditure was, and remains, substantial by Chinese standards. It is therefore reasonable to regard the educational environment in which I grew up as substantially more privileged than that experienced by the majority of Chinese children.

My family’s relationship with the international information environment also exposed me to aspects of Chinese society that might otherwise have remained invisible to a child. I became aware of the existence of China’s Internet censorship system at a relatively young age, partly because my father worked as an independent digital music producer. Much of the information and technical knowledge relevant to his work necessarily originated outside China. Digital music production was still a relatively undeveloped field in China during the period in which he entered it, only a little more than a decade after the beginning of the Reform and Opening-Up period. In practical terms, he was therefore operating in a field whose technological and cultural foundations were substantially connected to the international environment. The significance of these circumstances is not that they somehow made me immune to the problems of Chinese society. Rather, they provided me with a set of experiences that made certain contradictions visible at an unusually early stage. Exposure to international information networks, a relatively privileged educational environment, and a family whose professional activities required engagement with knowledge originating outside mainland China collectively created opportunities to recognize aspects of Chinese society that might otherwise have remained outside my field of awareness. In retrospect, I believe that this partial awareness was one of the factors that enabled me to recognize certain structural dangers before they became personally consequential.

Yet there is an important paradox here. Even individuals such as myself—and even some of my former friends, whose families were, in certain respects, wealthier than my own—could not entirely escape what I perceive as the systemic pressures and exploitative tendencies embedded within the Chinese educational system. If students situated within comparatively privileged families and comparatively well-resourced schools could still be subjected to these institutional dynamics, then the situation faced by the much larger population of students attending public schools with fewer educational resources and, in many cases, more conventional educational philosophies should be expected to be no less serious. On the contrary, the structural problems of the system would presumably become more visible under conditions in which institutional resources, pedagogical alternatives, and parental capacity to compensate for deficiencies were more limited. This becomes particularly apparent in the collection of testimonies discussed above by the overseas Chinese-language commentator whom I referred to earlier as “Lao Zhou.” His video described an open call for accounts of problems within Chinese education, to which he reportedly received numerous submissions from parents, students, teachers, and former students. The geographical diversity of these testimonies, as well as their strikingly similar descriptions of educational practices, provides a useful point of comparison with my own experience. The school I attended was certainly not characterized by the most extreme forms of corporal punishment described in some of these accounts, nor did it impose conditions as severe as systematically preventing students from bathing. Nevertheless, when one examines the underlying logic through which educational activities were organized, the similarities become considerably more difficult to dismiss. I will return to this point in greater detail below.

There is, however, another difficulty that arises when I attempt to use my own childhood as evidence: memory itself is an unstable source. When I deliberately attempt to reconstruct my memories of primary school, I frequently experience something resembling the recollection of a dream—something vivid, coherent, and emotionally recognizable, yet strangely distant from ordinary reality. Psychological research has long established that autobiographical memory is not a fixed record of past events, but is continually reconstructed in relation to the passage of time, subsequent experiences, and changes in personal identity. I therefore cannot assume that every detail of my recollection is historically precise. Nevertheless, even after taking this limitation into account, I do not believe that the broader conclusion I draw from these memories is simply an artifact of retrospective distortion. My primary-school experience, as I remember it, was in many respects genuinely positive.

The experience of primary school, however, is not itself the object of my criticism. Its significance lies elsewhere: it provides a contrast through which the fundamentally different educational logic I encountered after entering junior secondary school becomes more visible. Without this contrast, the transformation I experienced would be difficult to describe as a change in educational philosophy rather than merely a change in age, academic difficulty, or institutional environment. Shortly after graduating from primary school and entering junior secondary education, my entire conceptual environment underwent an extraordinarily rapid transformation. Within a matter of weeks, I could no longer recognize the educational philosophy that had characterized much of my primary-school experience: the cultivation of individual abilities, respect for students as developing persons, and an emphasis on etiquette, cooperation, and forms of personal development extending beyond academic performance. In its place appeared an entirely different set of messages. These messages did not originate from a single identifiable authority. No particular teacher, parent, or administrator sat me down and explicitly explained what I was expected to believe. Rather, the same basic proposition appeared to emerge simultaneously from the Internet, the school environment, and my family. Their cumulative effect was therefore considerably more powerful than any individual statement could have been. The proposition was simple, although to a twelve-year-old it was almost frightening in its implications: I had entered an arena governed by something resembling “natural selection,” and there was apparently no meaningful route of escape.
