As I have already suggested, for a child in his early teens who is approaching the beginning of junior secondary education, information originating from beyond the immediate social environment occupies remarkably little space in everyday life. The child is embedded first and foremost within the family; the family, in turn, must adapt to the material and institutional conditions of the society in which it exists; and the broader society itself operates within a political environment shaped by the mobilizational imperatives of the Chinese Communist Party. The individual therefore does not encounter society in its entirety. Rather, he encounters a succession of increasingly larger environments, each of which mediates his relationship with the next. In the specific case examined here, the individual is myself. What I experienced during adolescence was consequently not an isolated sequence of personal events, but a set of experiences already structured, to varying degrees, by the social environment into which I had been born. Before describing what happened to me personally, however, I believe it is necessary to draw an important inference concerning the educational system as a whole: how can the structure of the whole be inferred from the experience of a particular part? This is not merely a methodological question. It is central to understanding why an individual autobiography can possess analytical significance beyond the individual who experienced it. My experience cannot, by itself, establish the characteristics of the Chinese educational system as a whole. It can, however, serve as an entry point through which the institutional logic of that system becomes observable at the level of everyday life.

The first problem I encountered upon entering junior secondary school was the obvious increase in academic demands. The number of subjects increased substantially. In primary school, my formal curriculum had consisted primarily of Chinese, mathematics, and English. Beginning in Grade 7, however, students were also required to study history and Ethics and Rule of Law (the official name of a subject). Physics was added in Grade 8, followed by chemistry in Grade 9. The precise composition of the junior-secondary curriculum varies somewhat across provinces and municipalities. The list I present here corresponds to the subjects included in the senior high school entrance examination (the Zhongkao), in one district of Wuhan, the specific identity of which I omit for privacy reasons. The examination subjects are those whose results contribute directly to the student’s final examination score. To the best of my understanding, Wuhan is unusual in that biology and geography are not included among the subjects assessed in the senior high school entrance examination. In the district where I studied, this resulted in a total of seven examination subjects. In jurisdictions where geography and biology are also included, a student preparing for the examination may therefore be required to study and simultaneously prepare for approximately seven to nine major subjects. I do not possess sufficiently detailed knowledge of the examination arrangements in every province and municipality to determine precisely which subjects may be excluded or added in different jurisdictions, and I therefore do not intend this figure as a universal description of the Chinese system. It is sufficient for present purposes to note the scale of the academic burden. Nor did these subjects exhaust the requirements imposed upon students in my district. Practical examinations in physics and chemistry contributed to the final score, while physical education was also incorporated into the assessment system. Taken together, these requirements meant that a junior-secondary student could reasonably find himself preparing for approximately ten distinct areas of assessed performance. Whether such an educational burden is defensible when compared with other countries characterized by highly competitive educational systems is a separate question, and not one that I intend to settle here. What I can say with considerably greater confidence is that requiring a child in his early teens to devote virtually the entirety of three formative years to a large collection of examination-oriented subjects carries an unmistakably heavy human cost.

The expansion of the curriculum consequently leads to a second problem: the compression of time itself. According to my recollection, I was required to wake at approximately 6:30 a.m. each morning because my school operated on a residential basis. By 7:20, students were expected to be in their classrooms for morning study. This meant that within approximately fifty minutes, we had to wake, dress, wash, eat breakfast in the cafeteria, and then make our way to the classroom. At my school, the dormitories and cafeteria were relatively close to one another, but the cafeteria and classrooms were separated by a long underground passage, so the final portion of the journey itself consumed a nontrivial amount of time. Morning study began at approximately 7:30 and continued until 7:50. After a brief ten-minute break, the first class of the day began. Each class lasted approximately forty minutes, followed by a break of slightly more than ten minutes. At around 10:30, there was a longer recess of approximately thirty minutes. During this period, students might receive a small snack, usually consisting of desserts or similar food. Depending on the day, the grade level might also organize a flag-raising ceremony, mandatory on Monday mornings unless weather conditions prevented it, or physical activities such as organized running or skipping rope. Such activities became particularly important as the senior high school entrance examination approached, because physical education itself constituted part of the examination. Classes then resumed and continued until approximately 12:20 p.m. Students would rush to the cafeteria for lunch and were nominally expected to return to the dormitories for midday rest by 12:50. My school did not strictly compel students to sleep during this period. Some students would instead play basketball or engage in other activities, although they could be reprimanded by teachers if they were discovered. At approximately 1:40 p.m., dormitory supervisors would wake the students, and the first afternoon class began at 2:00 p.m. Classes continued until approximately 5:00 p.m., at which point students went to the cafeteria for dinner. Evening study, although nominally described as “self-study,” was in practice essentially another series of formal classes. It began at approximately 6:30 p.m. and consisted of four evening study periods, each lasting approximately one hour. The schedule therefore continued until approximately 9:30 p.m. During Grade 9, the evening schedule could be extended by another hour, with classes continuing until approximately 10:30 p.m. Only then could students return to the dormitories, where they still had to shower, complete whatever remained of their work, and sleep. The following morning, the cycle began again.

If the reader is inclined to regard such a schedule as already exceeding the reasonable limits of what a student should be expected to endure, I should emphasize that this is not, in fact, the principal evidence upon which I base my criticism of Chinese education. Quite the opposite is true. If the defining characteristic of Chinese education were merely its demanding schedule, it would not, in my view, constitute a sufficient basis for severe criticism. From a macro-level perspective, demanding schedules are hardly unique to China; many developed societies impose substantial temporal and performance demands upon both workers and students. From a micro-level perspective, moreover, my own experience provides an important qualification. Despite the intensity of the environment described above, most of the students I knew were still capable of reaching the end of junior secondary school with a relatively healthy and life-affirming sense of youth. This is possible because, for individuals who have spent their formative years within an examination-oriented educational system, many forms of pressure eventually become normalized. Frequent examinations, seemingly endless assignments, the occupation of recess periods, the appropriation of physical-education classes for academic instruction, the public ranking of students as either high or low performers, the erosion of weekends and holidays to periods of only limited free time, the reduction of summer and winter vacations to only a little more than twenty days, and the accelerated teaching of material belonging to later semesters are not experienced as exceptional violations of the ordinary educational order. They become ordinary features of everyday student life.

Academic pressure alone, therefore, is not necessarily sufficient to destroy a young person’s attachment to life. Students can still whisper to one another during class, copy a classmate’s homework after school, exchange small gestures when ordered to stand as punishment and then struggle not to laugh, and remain remarkably energetic during breaks, talking, joking, and insulting one another with the exaggerated intensity characteristic of adolescence. They can exchange gossip about who is dating whom, or sit together during meals and discuss the video games they play in their spare time. This is where a more important observation emerges: some of the most valuable human emotions arise precisely outside the rules through which institutional life attempts to organize them. These students were not necessarily exceptionally well educated in any broader sense. Nor were they necessarily talented, cultivated, or even particularly admirable. Some of the people I encountered during those years were individuals whom I found deeply unpleasant. Yet if the immediate frictions of everyday life are temporarily set aside, I still find myself wishing that these people could become something more than the categories imposed upon them by an examination system: independent and authentic human beings capable of thinking, loving, hating, reflecting upon the world around them, and ultimately forming judgments of their own.

It was while writing this section that I became aware of another problem. The highly restrictive, closed, and quasi-militarized management structures that I had come to associate with a substantial portion of the Chinese educational system had, to a significant extent, been absent from my own experience. In some schools, students did not wake at 6:30 a.m. but at 5:30 a.m.[^1] Nor did they necessarily begin the day by sitting in a classroom for silent reading. Instead, they might be required to assemble on the athletic field and participate in organized running exercises immediately after waking. The most frequently cited example is perhaps Hengshui High School, where various highly restrictive practices have become emblematic of examination-oriented educational discipline. Students have reportedly been prohibited from raising their heads during self-study periods, with violations subject to public criticism. Conversation while waiting in the cafeteria may be prohibited, and students may be expected to use the time to read textbooks aloud rather than engage in ordinary conversation. Practices have even extended to regulating personal routines such as hair washing on the grounds that doing so constitutes an inefficient use of time. Whether every such practice was implemented uniformly, continuously, or precisely as described in public accounts is a question requiring separate empirical verification. What matters for the present argument is the existence of an educational model in which the maximization of examination-related productivity can penetrate deeply into the ordinary routines of students’ private lives.

My own school was considerably less extreme. Such explicitly codified restrictions were largely absent. The pressure instead operated through a less formal mechanism: there was simply too much work to complete within the officially allocated time, and students therefore had to sacrifice their own leisure and personal routines. If an examination was scheduled for the following class, students might secretly carry small study booklets with them while walking to the athletic field so that they could continue memorizing material. Others would return to the dormitory and continue working on assignments, sometimes to the point that they simply decided not to shower that evening. At least at the level of explicit institutional rules, therefore, the system in which I studied was not coercive in the same manner. Students retained a certain degree of discretion. They could make choices about how to allocate their limited time, even if the structure surrounding those choices strongly constrained the available alternatives. This may appear to be a minor distinction, but I have come to regard it as one of the most important distinctions in my own experience. When formal rules retain a certain degree of flexibility, students are implicitly permitted a greater degree of individual discretion. They are not necessarily granted freedom in any substantive political sense; rather, they are permitted to negotiate, within narrow boundaries, how they will respond to institutional demands.

Yet this distinction also reveals its own limitation. The existence of relatively relaxed formal rules did not mean that my school had adopted an entirely different educational logic or value system. It represented a compromise between the demands of the examination regime and the residual autonomy of students; it did not constitute a transition to an alternative conception of education. The system had not returned to anything resembling the relative openness and intrinsic orientation toward the child that I associate, perhaps nostalgically, with the earliest stages of schooling. The difference was therefore one of degree and institutional expression rather than one of fundamental educational philosophy. This realization also helps explain something that initially appeared to me as unusual in my own intellectual development. When I first watched Lao Zhou’s discussion of Chinese education, I experienced almost no sense of surprise. This reaction is significant precisely because Lao Zhou was describing some of the darkest features of the Chinese public-school system: forms of discipline, surveillance, temporal control, and educational pressure that appeared to me, at least superficially, far removed from the school environment in which I had grown up. Why, then, was I not shocked?

The question becomes more puzzling when one considers my own background. I had grown up in a private international school rather than within the most extreme forms of the Chinese public-school environment. If a student who had never physically lived under such conditions could nevertheless encounter a description of them without experiencing substantial surprise, then there are at least two possible explanations. The first is that my psychological development or prior exposure to information had somehow made me unusually receptive to such descriptions. But there is another possibility that cannot be dismissed so easily: perhaps I had already experienced the darkness being described, even though I had never physically occupied its most extreme institutional form. This possibility is where my personal experience becomes analytically significant. The issue is not simply that different Chinese schools impose different degrees of academic pressure. Nor is it sufficient to distinguish between “good” schools and “bad” schools according to the severity of their schedules or disciplinary rules. The more fundamental question concerns the extent to which a particular institutional logic can be reproduced across environments that appear, on the surface, to be substantially different from one another. My school was not Hengshui. I did not experience the most extreme form of militarized educational discipline. I was not subjected to every restriction that can be found in the most rigid examination-oriented schools. Yet many of the underlying imperatives remained recognizable: academic performance was treated as the central organizing principle; time was continuously converted into measurable educational productivity; leisure was subordinated to examination preparation; and students were expected to internalize the assumption that the sacrifice of their own time, comfort, and autonomy was simply part of what successful education required.

The significance of this distinction is therefore not that my experience proves the uniformity of Chinese education. It does not. Rather, it suggests that institutional influence need not take the form of explicit coercion in order to become socially effective. A system may operate through formal rules in one environment, through informal expectations in another, and through self-imposed discipline in a third. What remains constant is not necessarily the visible form of control, but the underlying hierarchy of values through which individuals learn to understand what their time is for, what constitutes success, and what kinds of personal sacrifice are considered normal. This is also why the boundary between my own experience and the educational environments that initially appeared foreign to me was less substantial than I had once assumed. The difference lay not in whether the examination regime existed, but in how far its logic had been permitted to penetrate the ordinary life of the student. And it is precisely here that the broader argument of this part of the book begins to take shape. Institutions do not need to reproduce themselves identically in every local environment in order to produce broadly comparable forms of socialization. What matters is the transmission of the underlying logic. Once that logic becomes sufficiently normalized, individuals may reproduce its demands without requiring constant direct coercion. The institution no longer needs to command every action explicitly; individuals begin to anticipate what the institution expects of them and organize their own behavior accordingly. Looking back, this may be the most important reason why Lao Zhou’s account did not strike me as shocking. I had not lived through precisely the same institutional environment that he described. But I had already encountered enough of the same underlying logic to recognize what I was seeing. The darkness, in other words, did not necessarily begin where the most extreme rules began. It could already exist in a much more ordinary environment—one in which the rules appeared reasonable, the pressure appeared normal, and the compromises appeared voluntary. And this is where my personal history is about to continue.
