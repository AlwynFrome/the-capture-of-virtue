Looking back from the present, I have come to recognize a disturbing consequence of the process described in the preceding section. The most dangerous form of discipline is not necessarily the one imposed most visibly or most violently. It may instead be the one that succeeds so completely in becoming part of the individual’s own system of judgment that the distinction between external coercion and personal choice begins to disappear. By the time discipline had become normalized within me, I no longer needed to be instructed to work harder, to sacrifice leisure, or to suppress fatigue. I had learned to perform these functions myself. In this sense, the catastrophe that followed was not produced by coercion alone. It was, in a more complicated sense, a catastrophe in which I had become a participant. This does not mean that I regard myself as morally responsible for the conditions that produced my suffering. Rather, it means that the institutional logic had become sufficiently internalized that I could reproduce its demands without anyone having to reproduce them on my behalf.

This process had begun long before I entered junior secondary school. From an early age, I was unusually sensitive to the opinions of others and to the expectations embedded within my immediate social environment. I could readily perceive what parents, teachers, and other adults considered desirable behavior, even when those expectations were never explicitly articulated. At the same time, I possessed comparatively little sense of an autonomous identity independent of external evaluation. The combination was consequential. I was not particularly interested in asserting myself against other people, but I was highly capable of adjusting myself to what I perceived they wanted me to become. In primary school, this tendency was continuously rewarded. Adults praised the obedient and academically successful child; the “good student” was treated as a desirable social type; and academic excellence provided a particularly clear and measurable basis upon which approval could be granted. I therefore learned not simply that studying was valuable, but that being the kind of person who studied successfully was itself a valuable form of existence. What other people admired gradually became what I expected of myself.

The transition into junior secondary school intensified this process because the broader ideological framework described earlier now acquired a personal and behavioral form. I increasingly accepted, almost without conscious examination, the proposition that extraordinary effort would produce extraordinary outcomes, that academic achievement constituted a responsibility toward my family and my future, and that failure to exert sufficient effort might lead to consequences that could not later be reversed. I believed these propositions for different reasons, but they converged upon the same behavioral conclusion: I had to continue. The promise that effort would eventually produce security made sacrifice appear rational; the language of responsibility transformed achievement into a moral obligation; and the loss of the identity of the “good student” threatened the very basis upon which I understood myself. Most importantly, I was frightened by the possibility of insufficient effort. I could not have explained precisely what disaster would follow if I stopped, nor could I have established that such a disaster actually existed. Yet the uncertainty itself did not weaken the fear. It strengthened it. When a child is taught that failure may produce an irreversible catastrophe but is not given the conceptual tools necessary to evaluate that claim, the rational response may become indistinguishable from permanent vigilance.

What followed was an increasingly extreme form of self-regulation. From the beginning of junior secondary school until the end of Grade 9, I repeatedly used conscious willpower to override the ordinary signals produced by my body. When the alarm rang in the morning, I would force myself out of bed almost immediately, often becoming fully alert within seconds. I would complete breakfast quickly and frequently arrive at the classroom before my classmates. At night, even after completing all assignments formally required by my teachers, I would continue working on additional materials that I had purchased for myself. There was no particular authority standing behind me at these moments. No teacher needed to remain in the room. No parent needed to supervise the number of pages I completed. I had already become my own supervisor. The resulting academic performance was correspondingly extraordinary: apart from the later period in which my deteriorating psychological condition caused me to fall to second place in my class, I ranked first in virtually every major comprehensive examination throughout junior secondary school and remained almost continuously within the top fifty students at the grade level. Yet the significance of these results is not simply that I performed well. It is that, viewed from the perspective of an ordinary classmate, the behavioral pattern itself could appear remarkably obsessive. I was, in effect, becoming the kind of “top student” whose existence is difficult to understand unless one assumes an unusually extreme hierarchy of values—one in which academic ranking justifies the systematic sacrifice of leisure, comfort, social interaction, and eventually psychological stability. What makes the experience difficult to reconstruct even now is that I did not experience myself as an extremist. From inside the system, the behavior had become ordinary. What might have appeared to another student as pathological dedication was experienced by me as nothing more than what a responsible student was supposed to do.

This distinction also explains why it would be misleading to interpret my behavior simply as ambition. I did not study because I wished to dominate my classmates or acquire the privilege of looking down upon them. Academic superiority was not, in itself, the object I desired. Indeed, one of my strongest and most enduring desires has been to find people with whom I can speak as an intellectual equal and experience genuine mutual understanding. The mechanism that kept me studying was therefore less straightforward than competitive ambition. It was recognition. I was surrounded by people who praised me, admired my achievements, regarded me as an example, and derived a sense of pride from my performance. Yet the person they were responding to was largely an institutional identity: the successful student, the disciplined student, the exemplary student. The fear and responsibility beneath that identity were considerably less visible. This created a feedback loop in which achievement produced recognition, recognition reinforced the identity of the successful student, and the preservation of that identity required continued achievement. Once this loop had been internalized, stopping became psychologically expensive. To reduce my effort was not merely to study less. It threatened the basis upon which I had learned to understand my own worth. If the external image of the “good student” were removed, I did not know what remained. Beneath the reputation was an exhausted, frightened adolescent, and I had never learned to regard that person as possessing an independent claim to value.

The responses of the adults around me made this problem more visible precisely because they were not uniformly cruel. Most people did not appear to understand what was happening beneath the surface, and I do not think that every individual involved consciously intended to produce it. When I attempted to tell my mother about my suffering, she cried and asked, “You are throwing all of this emotional garbage at me, but who am I supposed to throw it to?” When my academic performance declined, a former homeroom teacher responded by reminding me that I could not afford to fall behind. When I eventually became too exhausted to continue and decided to take a voluntary leave from school, I returned home to anger and verbal abuse rather than the patient care I had hoped for. There was, however, one small exception that has remained unusually significant in my memory. The dormitory supervisor told me that I could not continue fighting a “war of exhaustion” and that I needed to rest when rest was necessary. Her statement was simple, but precisely for that reason it revealed the structural problem. An individual could tell me to rest, yet the surrounding system had already taught me that rest was difficult to distinguish from surrender. The rhetoric of improving one’s score by one point and thereby surpassing thousands of competitors did more than encourage effort. It also implied that every moment spent resting was potentially a moment in which another student could gain an advantage. The student was therefore placed in a peculiar psychological situation: even when no authority explicitly prohibited rest, the competitive structure itself could make rest feel irresponsible. Under such conditions, the institution did not need to command every act of sacrifice. The student could perform the sacrifice voluntarily and then interpret that voluntariness as evidence of personal virtue.

The consequences eventually became severe. Prolonged exhaustion was followed by serious depression, and the distinction between deliberate self-control and psychological destabilization became increasingly difficult to maintain. During a period of recovery at home, I experienced several incidents involving memory and behavior that I have never been able to explain satisfactorily. I do not claim that these experiences establish a particular psychiatric diagnosis, nor can I retrospectively determine with confidence what mechanism produced them. What I can say is that there were occasions on which my recollection of events appeared fundamentally incompatible with what I was subsequently told had happened. On one occasion, I was told that my grandmother had asked whether I wanted to eat dumplings and that I had responded impatiently, telling her to go away. I had no memory of the conversation, and the reported behavior seemed inconsistent with how I understood my own ordinary conduct toward people close to me. On another occasion, during an argument with my mother, I recorded approximately an hour of our conversation on my phone. One statement remained particularly vivid in my memory: “If you want to kill yourself, then go ahead. I cannot be bothered to deal with it.” I intended to keep the recording privately as evidence. Yet at some later point, I discovered that the recording had appeared in the WeChat conversation between my mother and me, apparently sent from my own account. I had no memory of sending it and could identify no reason why I would have done so. Because I had always regarded my memory as unusually strong, the incident was particularly difficult to reconcile. At the time, I could only speculate that prolonged psychological pressure had begun to interfere with my ability to maintain a coherent sense of consciousness and self. I even wondered whether some part of my mind had attempted to create an alternative psychological outlet through which accumulated fear, anger, and suffering could be expressed. Whether that interpretation was correct remains uncertain. The more defensible conclusion is simply that the system of self-control I had relied upon for years had ceased to function as a stable mechanism of adaptation.

This is where the title of this chapter acquires its intended meaning. The catastrophe was “complicit” not because the victim willingly consented to being harmed, but because the mechanisms that produced the harm had become distributed across so many levels that no single actor was sufficient to explain the outcome. The educational system supplied the competitive structure. Motivational discourse supplied the emotional vocabulary. Family expectations supplied moral significance. Academic rankings supplied measurable confirmation. Social recognition supplied reinforcement. And I supplied the final mechanism through which all of these demands were translated into continuous self-discipline. The result was a form of coercion that increasingly appeared as personal choice. This is also why the distinction established in the preceding section between explicit institutional discipline and self-imposed discipline is so important. A student may be free, in the narrow sense, to decide whether to study for another hour. Yet if the student has already internalized the belief that stopping constitutes irresponsibility, weakness, or betrayal, the formal existence of choice tells us very little about the substantive freedom of that decision. The institution has succeeded not when it prevents the student from resting, but when the student prevents himself.

Eventually, I left the immediate school environment and entered a study-abroad preparatory institution, living alone in Beijing. Paradoxically, this became both the darkest and the most consequential period of my life. My suicidal ideation was at its most intense during this period, and I came closer to death than at any other point in my life. It was also the period in which I most extensively circumvented the restrictions of the mainland Chinese Internet and encountered information, historical accounts, and intellectual perspectives that had previously remained outside my field of awareness. More importantly, it was the beginning of a process through which I gradually separated the values I had actually chosen from those I had merely inherited. For years, I had treated recognition as evidence of worth, achievement as evidence of identity, and endurance as evidence of virtue. Distance from the immediate educational environment did not instantly undo these assumptions. Institutional habits and psychological adaptations do not disappear simply because an individual changes location. Yet the separation made them visible. I began to recognize that the person I had been trained to become was not necessarily the person I wanted to be. In this sense, my eventual arrival in Singapore represented more than a geographical transition. It created the distance from which I could begin to examine my own past as an object of inquiry rather than merely as the environment in which I was expected to continue living.

I therefore return to the paradox with which this chapter began. Looking back, some of the qualities that had been most consistently rewarded in me—discipline, perseverance, responsibility, academic excellence, and the capacity to endure hardship—were not inherently false virtues. The problem was that they had been detached from any meaningful conception of human limitation. They were transformed from capacities into obligations, from obligations into identities, and from identities into mechanisms of self-coercion. I was praised because I could endure; I learned to endure because I was praised; and eventually I became unable to stop enduring even when endurance itself had become destructive. The educational system did not simply teach me how to study. It taught me how to evaluate myself, how to interpret fatigue, how to understand failure, and ultimately how to treat my own limits as obstacles that ought to be overcome. The catastrophe was therefore not an accidental deviation from the system’s logic. It was, at least in part, the consequence of that logic functioning with extraordinary efficiency.

I did not emerge from this process unchanged. I emerged from it with a much more skeptical understanding of the relationship between virtue and obedience, effort and worth, discipline and coercion. I also emerged with a conviction that becomes important for the remainder of this Part: the most consequential effects of an educational system cannot be measured solely by examination results. They must also be examined through the kinds of lives that the system makes possible, the kinds of lives it renders difficult, and the kinds of sacrifices it teaches individuals to regard as acceptable. My experience represents only one trajectory. Others will be different. Some students will adapt successfully to the same competitive structure; some will resist it; some will be protected by family resources, institutional alternatives, or psychological resilience; others will bear costs that remain invisible until much later. The system therefore does not produce a single uniform human outcome. It produces a field of differentiated possibilities in which individuals, unequally positioned and unequally equipped, are exposed to the same underlying logic in radically different ways.

That is where the argument must now move.

They may have taught me to become a machine capable of surviving anything. For a time, I almost succeeded.

But I remain here.
