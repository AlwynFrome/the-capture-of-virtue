At this point in the argument, we can finally connect the alienating effects of the educational system to the political emotions and nationalist values discussed in Part I. In Section 2.1.2, I described how the educational system can systematically cultivate individuals who are highly responsive to political mobilization while possessing a weakened capacity for independent judgment. But that account describes only one possible trajectory. It does not yet show what happens to the rest of the population. If one has lived inside Chinese society while spending substantial time moving between the Simplified Chinese Internet and the Chinese-language Internet beyond it, a broader pattern begins to emerge. The individuals encountered within these different environments are obviously not identical. They differ in education, social position, political awareness, ambition, temperament, access to information, and willingness to confront political questions. Yet these differences do not make the system unintelligible. On the contrary, once these differences are placed alongside the institutional conditions that shape them, a recognizable pattern begins to appear. I have tried to represent that pattern in Figure 2.1-2.

The figure requires some explanation, because without it the branches can easily be misunderstood. It is not intended as a rigid taxonomy of Chinese people, nor does it suggest that every individual must pass through a fixed sequence of stages. It is better understood as a map of possible trajectories: different paths that become more or less likely when individual differences interact with the institutional environment in which people are educated and socialized. At this point, I need to introduce one concept that will become important for interpreting the figure: Political Intelligence.

By Political Intelligence, I mean the capacity of an individual to perceive political reality with sufficient independence to recognize structures of power, distinguish political argument from emotional mobilization, identify distortions in public discourse, and revise one’s judgment when confronted with inconvenient evidence. The concept is deliberately narrower than intelligence in the conventional sense. A person may be exceptionally intelligent in mathematics, engineering, medicine, business, or literature while displaying remarkably poor judgment when confronted with political questions. This distinction matters because political judgment is not merely a cognitive exercise. To understand a political question, an individual may have to recognize an interest that conflicts with one’s own, acknowledge a historical fact that threatens one’s identity, question an institution upon which one’s social position depends, or accept a conclusion that may carry consequences for one’s family, employment, reputation, or physical safety. The obstacle to accurate judgment is therefore not necessarily a lack of intelligence. Sometimes the obstacle is the cost of using it.

Consider a simple example. Suppose you become fascinated by the internal structure of an apple. At first, your curiosity is uncomplicated. You observe its color and shape, taste it, read about it, and perhaps eventually study biology to understand its microscopic composition. But imagine that every attempt to discuss the subject with others is met with hostility. Every disagreement becomes a personal attack. Every question is interpreted as provocation. Every independent opinion carries some social cost. Eventually, you may simply stop asking questions. If someone subsequently asks you about the microscopic structure of an apple, you might offer only some superficial answer about DNA, or perhaps nothing at all. Not because you have lost the intellectual capacity to understand biology, but because you no longer wish to enter the conflict surrounding intellectual inquiry. This distinction is particularly important in political life. A person’s most revealing political judgment may not be the judgment that would emerge under conditions of perfect safety, but the deepest judgment that remains possible under the constraints the person actually faces. Political Intelligence therefore concerns not simply what a person could understand in the abstract, but what quality of political judgment remains available when fear, social pressure, material dependence, or the prospect of punishment are part of the environment itself. The labels in Figure 2.1-2 should therefore be understood in this sense. They do not describe fixed types of human beings. They describe different relationships between intellectual capacity, political awareness, and the willingness or ability to act upon what one perceives.

Once this distinction is established, the tree reveals several things. First, China’s elites are selected rather than simply produced. This is perhaps the first point that needs to be made clear. The branching structure represented in the figure should not be understood as a detailed plan consciously designed by the Chinese Communist Party. Such a claim would attribute to the Party a degree of foresight and administrative control that the argument does not require. Selection can occur without explicit planning. Institutions can systematically reward some dispositions, suppress others, and create predictable distributions of outcomes without any central authority having designed every branch in advance. But this qualification does not make the political implications insignificant. On the contrary, it makes them more interesting. A political system that depends upon obedience and political mobilizability has little reason to systematically cultivate precisely those intellectual dispositions that make independent judgment possible: curiosity unconstrained by political boundaries, willingness to question authority, tolerance for contradiction, and the confidence to pursue an inconvenient conclusion simply because the evidence appears to require it.

The CCP has already witnessed what can happen when such dispositions become politically consequential. In the spring and early summer of 1989, students demanding political reform did not emerge from a vacuum. Many of them acted from a sense of responsibility toward their country and expressed strong criticism of the political and economic conditions around them. Whatever complexities one assigns to the movement, and however one distributes responsibility for the events that followed, one fact is difficult to ignore: a society in which independent and critical political consciousness becomes sufficiently widespread can eventually produce demands that threaten the existing distribution of political power. Since then, political openness in China has not developed in a direction that would suggest an institutional commitment to cultivating such independence as a political virtue. This creates a structural contradiction. The system may have every reason to want capable people. It wants scientists who can win international recognition, athletes who can bring prestige, engineers who can build technologically sophisticated infrastructure, entrepreneurs who can generate economic value, and intellectuals who can demonstrate the achievements of Chinese society. It has an obvious interest in excellence when excellence can be converted into national prestige. But excellence itself has requirements. If one wants a person to become genuinely exceptional, one must allow that person to discover where their own abilities lie. One must encourage them to identify problems that others have overlooked, ask questions that have not yet been answered, and develop solutions that do not merely reproduce existing answers. These are precisely the dispositions associated with intellectual independence and critical inquiry.

The contradiction therefore cannot simply be solved by producing people who are simultaneously highly capable and permanently politically unquestioning. A person can be politically silent while remaining intellectually capable. A person can choose not to challenge an existing political conclusion because the consequences are too costly. But silence is not the same as intellectual incapacity. A genuinely capable thinker may refrain from asking a politically dangerous question without losing the ability to recognize that the question exists. This is why exceptional individuals should not necessarily be understood as straightforward products of the system that educated them. In some cases, their intellectual development may instead represent something that the system could exploit but could not fully contain. The system can reward their excellence. It can display their achievements as evidence of national strength. But the very qualities that make their achievements possible—independence, curiosity, specialization, problem-solving, and the willingness to discover what is actually true—can also give them the capacity to recognize contradictions within the political environment itself. This is the deeper paradox of an educational system that seeks both excellence and political conformity. It needs people who can think. It also needs people who will not think too far about the conditions under which they are being asked to think. And those two objectives are never entirely compatible.

Second, the “Chinese” in this system is not merely a nationality. It is an identity cultivated through education. Nationality can be formally assigned. Identity cannot. A person becomes accustomed to thinking of themselves as Chinese through a long process of socialization: language, historical narratives, geographical imagination, political symbols, education, family expectations, public commemorations, and repeated distinctions between “us” and “them.” A person who has not been exposed to these practices may still possess Chinese ancestry or citizenship, but they may not necessarily use “Chinese” as the primary framework through which they understand themselves. This is why the educational system matters in a way that extends beyond academic instruction. It does not merely teach a child mathematics, language, history, and science. It also teaches the child which historical events deserve remembrance, which political symbols deserve reverence, which territories are imagined as belonging to the national community, which foreign societies occupy the position of friend or adversary, and what it means to be a responsible member of the nation. The result is not simply an educated individual. It is a particular kind of political subject.

This does not mean that every Chinese person possesses the same beliefs. Clearly they do not. Nor does it mean that national identity is entirely artificial. People can develop genuine emotional attachments to language, family, landscape, history, and cultural traditions. The point is narrower and, I believe, more consequential: what is commonly treated as a natural and self-evident national identity is also the outcome of an extended process of social cultivation. The distinction matters because once an identity becomes deeply internalized, political claims can acquire a moral force that they would not otherwise possess. A question about territory becomes a question about national dignity. A question about history becomes a question about loyalty. A disagreement with the state becomes a possible betrayal of the nation. Political authority can therefore borrow legitimacy from an identity that individuals experience as their own. This is one reason education can exercise political influence without functioning as explicit propaganda at every moment. The most effective forms of political socialization do not require a person to consciously repeat a political doctrine throughout their life. They only need to establish the boundaries within which certain conclusions feel natural and others feel unthinkable.

Third, whatever kind of person emerges from this process, the political order can often make use of the result. This, to me, is the most unsettling part of the entire tree. The branches point in very different directions. Some people become politically foolish: they possess considerable education and intellectual ability but have difficulty applying critical judgment to political questions. They may become enthusiastic supporters of the regime and active participants in nationalist mobilization. Some become politically numb. They may have little interest in political abstraction, historical grievances, or national causes. They do not necessarily oppose the system. They simply withdraw from the domain in which political mobilization operates. Some become politically intelligent but remain silent. They may understand the structure of power, recognize contradictions, and privately disagree with the official narrative, while concluding that public confrontation is too costly. Some attempt to circumvent censorship and search for information beyond the boundaries of the domestic information environment. Some openly rebel. They speak, organize, publish, protest, or otherwise attempt to challenge political authority. Some leave the country and become critics from abroad.

At first glance, these trajectories appear radically different. They are different in belief, knowledge, behavior, and personal experience. It would therefore be tempting to arrange them along a simple spectrum from loyalty to opposition. I do not think that is sufficient. The more important question is not merely what people believe, but what their beliefs and circumstances allow them to do. A politically intelligent individual who recognizes the structure of power but remains silent may possess considerable private disagreement while generating almost no public disruption. The political system does not necessarily need to convince this person that it is legitimate. It may be enough to convince them that challenging it is too costly. An individual who openly dissents can be censored, deplatformed, detained, imprisoned, or otherwise marginalized. The immediate political effect of the dissent may therefore be limited. More importantly, the punishment itself communicates information to everyone else. It demonstrates not only that dissent exists, but what dissent costs. The same mechanism can operate through emigration. When politically critical individuals leave the country, their departure may remove a potential source of domestic opposition. Their criticism from abroad can then be incorporated into narratives of hostile foreign influence, foreign interference, or attempts to smear China. In the case of overseas Chinese, criticism can additionally be interpreted through the language of betrayal—of being a traitor, a sellout, or an enemy of the nation. The paradox is difficult to miss. The dissident may believe that they have escaped the system. Yet their existence abroad can be incorporated into the system’s narrative about the outside world. The emigrant can be transformed into evidence of foreign hostility. Even exceptional talent can be converted into national prestige without necessarily becoming political independence. So the tree does not ultimately describe a population in which everyone becomes the same. It describes something more sophisticated. The system does not need to make everyone alike. It needs to make difference politically manageable.

This is where the meaning of the tree becomes clearer. The branches should not be interpreted as a rigid taxonomy of Chinese people. They are possible trajectories generated by the interaction between individual differences and institutional constraints. The politically foolish may become active supporters of the regime. The politically numb may become politically irrelevant to it. The politically intelligent may become silent, marginalized, expatriated, or openly oppositional. Yet these different outcomes do not necessarily produce proportional differences in the stability of the political order. The convergence, then, is not a convergence of beliefs. It is a convergence of structural consequences. This may be one of the least intuitive features of authoritarian social reproduction. We tend to imagine political control as requiring uniformity: everyone must believe the same things, repeat the same slogans, and behave in the same way. But a sufficiently powerful political structure does not necessarily require uniform citizens. It may instead tolerate—and sometimes even absorb—substantial variation in individual attitudes, provided that such variation does not translate into an effective challenge to the underlying distribution of power. People may arrive at political loyalty through conviction, conformity, indifference, fear, material interest, exhaustion, or lack of information. These motivations are psychologically different and should not be collapsed into a single category. But from the perspective of political reproduction, their consequences may overlap. And this is precisely where the structure becomes disturbing. The political order does not necessarily defeat every form of difference. It can sometimes survive by incorporating difference itself.

Fourth, the people least affected by political mobilization may be those who never enter its field of operation. This point appears paradoxical at first. One might assume that the person most resistant to political influence would be the person with the greatest political awareness. But that is not necessarily so. A person with little interest in history, national identity, or political controversy may simply have withdrawn from the domain in which ideological influence operates. If historical grievances do not form part of that person’s conception of self, nationalist mobilization has little material upon which to act. If political questions are regarded as irrelevant to the immediate problems of survival, propaganda may be encountered without becoming psychologically consequential. In this limited sense, indifference can provide a form of protection that political sophistication does not. This is also why the distinction between intelligence and Political Intelligence matters so much. The politically numb may be less educated, less politically sophisticated, and less capable of articulating complex political arguments than the politically foolish. Yet the politically foolish may be much more deeply shaped by the political system precisely because they possess the intellectual resources to understand its concepts while lacking the independence to apply critical judgment to the institution that produced them. A highly educated young intellectual may understand Marxist theory, Chinese history, international relations, and sophisticated political concepts while remaining unable—or unwilling—to apply the same critical standards to the political institution that has taught them those concepts. A poorly educated migrant worker may possess none of this intellectual vocabulary and yet recognize, through direct experience, that political authority is exploiting them. The distinction is therefore not between intelligence and stupidity. It is between the ability to think and the ability to think politically about the conditions under which one’s thinking has been formed.

And this brings us back to education. The most consequential achievement of an educational system may not be that it tells people what to believe. A more powerful achievement is that it can influence what kinds of questions people regard as legitimate to ask, what kinds of evidence they regard as trustworthy, what kinds of sacrifices they regard as normal, and what kinds of authority they regard as beyond examination. Once these boundaries have been internalized, political control no longer depends entirely upon continuous coercion. Individuals begin to sort themselves. Some pursue achievement within the permitted boundaries. Some retreat into private life. Some learn to remain silent. Some search for information elsewhere. Some leave. Some rebel. Some become fervent defenders of the system. And some simply stop caring. Their lives diverge. Their beliefs diverge. Their levels of education diverge. Their political awareness diverges. Yet the existence of these differences does not necessarily imply the existence of effective political pluralism. This is, ultimately, what I believe Figure \ref{fig:divergent-convergent} is showing. The Chinese educational system does not simply produce one kind of person. It produces a population whose members can become remarkably different from one another. But those differences emerge within an institutional environment that determines the costs of inquiry, the rewards of conformity, the risks of dissent, the meaning of national identity, and the boundaries of legitimate thought. The result is therefore not uniformity. It is differentiation without effective political pluralism. People may become different without becoming politically consequential. And perhaps this is the deeper paradox of the system. It does not have to prevent every person from becoming intelligent. It does not have to prevent every person from becoming critical. It does not even have to prevent every person from becoming opposed to it. It only has to ensure that the paths by which people become intelligent, critical, indifferent, silent, oppositional, successful, or estranged do not readily converge upon an alternative distribution of political power.

If that is true, then the educational system has accomplished something considerably more sophisticated than indoctrination. It has not merely taught people what to think. It has helped shape how far they are willing to think, what they are willing to question, what they are willing to sacrifice, and what they believe those sacrifices mean. At that point, political control no longer has to stand outside the individual and command them. Part of it has already moved inside. The system does not need to decide every person’s destination. It only needs to shape the roads. And once the roads have been shaped, people can walk in very different directions while still remaining, in ways they may never fully perceive, within the same political landscape.

That, I believe, is the most unsettling implication of the tree.
